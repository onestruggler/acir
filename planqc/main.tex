\section{Introduction}
An equational theory for a family of quantum circuits is a set of equations between circuits used as rewrite rules. By applying these rules to subcircuits, we can transform a circuit into an equivalent one. Such a theory should be \emph{sound} because every rewrite preserves the underlying linear map. It should also be \emph{complete} so that any equivalence between circuits can be derived. Additionally, we want it to be \emph{minimal}, with no redundant rules. Equational rewriting supports circuit optimization~\cite{hietala2021voqc,nam2018automated,maslov2008simplification,xu2022quartz}, equivalence checking~\cite{yamashita2010fast}, and hardware mapping~\cite{sivarajah2020tket}. Complete theories also support reasoning about quantum programs. For example,
\mbox{Hadamard-$\Pi$}~\cite{fang2026hadamard} is a complete
equational theory for reversible quantum programs. Completeness results have been established for a wide range of circuit families, including reversible linear circuits~\cite{lafont2003boolean,sakashita2013cnot}, reversible circuits over multiple-control Toffoli gates~\cite{feng2026complete,feng2026ancillas}, Clifford~\cite{selinger2015clifford,makary2021real,li2025qutrit,bian2026qupit}, CNOT-dihedral~\cite{amy2018cnotdihedral,blake2026phaseaffine}, two-qubit Clifford+T~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2}, three-qubit Clifford+CS~\cite{bian2023cliffordcs3}, Toffoli--Hadamard~\cite{amy2024toffoli} and real Clifford+CH~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch} circuits, and with continuous parameters, all quantum circuits~\cite{clement2023complete,clement2024extensions}. Completeness has also been obtained for the group of two-level matrices underlying several of these circuit families~\cite{greylyn2014,bian2021un,li2021on,fang2026hadamard,clement2019thesis}. Minimal theories have been given for six of these circuit fragments~\cite{blake2026simpler} and for all quantum circuits~\cite{clement2024minimal}.

There are two main strategies for proving completeness of a rule set $\mathcal{R}$ over a gate set $\mathcal{G}$. Our machine-checked framework builds on both of them, so we start by sketching the ideas behind them. Consider any two circuits $C_1$ and $C_2$ over $\mathcal{G}$ that have the same linear map, $C_1\equiv_{u}C_2$. We need to show that $C_1$ can be rewritten into $C_2$ using $\mathcal{R}$. \Cref{fig:normalform} outlines the first strategy. It specifies a unique normal form for each linear map~\cite{lafont2003boolean,sakashita2013cnot,feng2026complete,feng2026ancillas,selinger2015clifford,bian2026qupit,makary2021real,amy2018cnotdihedral,blake2026phaseaffine,li2025qutrit}, often through giving an exact synthesis algorithm~\cite{greylyn2014,bian2021un,li2021on,fang2026hadamard,clement2019thesis}. Completeness follows by proving that every circuit can be transformed into its normal form using the rewrite rules. By uniqueness, $C_1$ and $C_2$ have the same normal form $N$. Reversing the path from $C_2$ to $N$ and concatenating it with the path from $C_1$ to $N$ gives
\[
C_1\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}N\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}C_2.
\]

\Cref{fig:translation} illustrates the second strategy, which uses an already complete rule set $\mathcal{R}'$ over a different gate set $\mathcal{G}'$~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2,bian2023cliffordcs3,clement2026realcliffordch,amy2024toffoli,blake2026simpler,clement2023complete,clement2024extensions,delorme2026control,schober2026control,blake2026qudit}. Here, $E$ and $D$ translate circuits from $\mathcal{G}$ to $\mathcal{G}'$ and back, replacing each gate with a circuit over the other gate set. Both $E$ and $D$ preserve circuit composition and the underlying linear map. To transfer completeness from $\mathcal{R}'$ to $\mathcal{R}$, we first show that every rule of $\mathcal{R}'$, translated by $D$, can be derived using $\mathcal{R}$. We can then replace each step of the proof between $C_1'$ and $C_2'$ by its translated derivation, giving $D(C_1')\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_2')$. We also need to check that each gate $g\in\mathcal{G}$ can be rewritten to its round-trip translation: $g\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(E(g))$. Applying these rewrites gate by gate gives $C_j\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_j')$ for $j\in\{1,2\}$. Combining these paths yields
\[
C_1\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_1')\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_2')\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}C_2.
\]
Sometimes the two methods are combined, as in the scaled dyadic extension of ~\cite{li2021on}.
 

Starting from a complete rule set, we can remove a redundant rule by deriving it from the other rules, thereby preserving completeness. To show that the resulting rule set is minimal, we need to give, for each remaining rule $R$, an interpretation of circuits that every other rule preserves but $R$ does not. Any derivation using the other rules preserves this interpretation, so $R$ cannot be derived from them~\cite{clement2024minimal,blake2026simpler}.

Completeness and minimality proofs typically require long case distinctions over gates, relations, and normal forms, whose supporting derivations sometimes span hundreds of pages~\cite{fang2026hadamard,li2025qutrit,clement2026realcliffordch}. In particular, relation reductions require intricate circuit manipulations and repeated rewrites, making them prone to human mistakes. For example, circular reasoning can lead us to remove a rule whose derivation still relies on that rule, hidden in a chain of other derivations. Errors can then propagate through dependent derivations, invalidating the entire reduction and making the error source hard to trace by hand. 

\paragraph{Our contributions.} To make completeness proofs robust and manageable as they extend to new gate sets, we turn to a machine-checked framework, where a proof assistant like Agda~\cite{norell2007thesis,bove2009agda} verifies every derivation and its dependencies. A human then only needs to check that the framework is set up correctly and the main theorems are stated as intended. Consistent definitions of circuits and their matrices also allow us to compare different completeness results in a unified setting. Because all proofs are checked at scale, such a framework paves the way for verifying circuit completeness in higher dimensions.

This work builds on our formalization of qudit Clifford completeness~\cite{bian2026qupit}, where we built the framework~\cite{bian2027popl} and carried out the main verification steps by hand. LLM coding agents came in only at the final stage, to connect various steps into the formal proof. Our experience was part of a broader shift where LLMs are now used to write large formal proofs. Anthropic, for example, reports that LLMs wrote a 13-million-line Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem within 11 days~\cite{anthropic2026flt}. In a hybrid approach, humans write and carefully review the definitions and theorem statements~\cite{hales2026reliability}, coding agents write the proofs, and Agda checks them. Following this, we extend our previous framework~\cite{bian2027popl} to QCert and use it to formalize completeness and minimality results from sixteen papers, as shown in \Cref{tab:corpus}.

\section{The QCert Framework}
\label{sec:form}

QCert states every theorem as a group presentation. A circuit is a word over an alphabet~$X$ of gates, and the rules are relations between words, which generate a congruence $\approx$. A semantics is a homomorphism $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ from words to a group $G$, such as a matrix group. Soundness and completeness say the relations present the image of $\llbracket - \rrbracket$, which is the subgroup of $G$ generated by the gates. Our earlier library~\cite{bian2027popl}, which QCert builds on, expresses this as a record. In Agda, the keyword \aid{record} defines mathematical structures. A group, for example, is a record. The record $\Gamma$~\aid{IsSubPresentationOf}~$G$ contains the semantics $\llbracket - \rrbracket$, with proofs of soundness and completeness. The record $\Gamma$~\aid{IsPresentationOf}~$G$ adds surjectivity, so the relations present all of $G$.

Unlike the letters of an ordinary word, gates act on wires. The gates in $X$ are indexed by the number of wires they act on. Wires are numbered from $0$ at the bottom, and a gate always acts on the bottom wires of a circuit. To place it anywhere else, we apply a shift, which takes a circuit on $n$ wires and places it on wires $1,\dots,n$ of a circuit with $n+1$ wires. Each relation is stated on as many wires as the paper draws it on. To apply it inside a circuit with more wires, we need \emph{structural rules}, which hold for every circuit, regardless of its gate set. These rules say that an equation can be shifted up by one wire, gates on disjoint wires commute, and a scalar can be placed on any wire. They are intuitive to humans and thus rarely stated, but a formalization in Agda must define them explicitly.

QCert then gives circuits a matrix semantics $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ with entries in one of the rings $\Z\left[1/\sqrt2,i\right]$, $\Z\left[1/2,i\right]$, $\Z\left[1/\sqrt2\right]$ and $\Z\left[1/2\right]$, which are used in exact synthesis algorithms. The target group $G$ is the unitary group over that ring. It is built from the group structures of Agda's standard library and defined once per ring, so it is checked once. As matrices, a gate on the bottom wires and a shift are Kronecker products with an identity, so the extension of $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ from gates to circuits is defined once for all gate sets. Since the structural rules are universal, their soundness is also proved only once. Then for each paper, it suffices to check the soundness of its own rules.

The formalization of each paper ends with its main theorem stated as a record of one of these two kinds, \aid{IsSubPresentationOf} or \aid{IsPresentationOf}. For example, \Cref{agda:stab} is the theorem for the real stabilizer circuits of~\cite{makary2021real}, with the relations R1--R16 of its Figure~1 lifted to $n$ wires. \Cref{agda:hpi} is the theorem for the two-level matrices of Hadamard-$\Pi$~\cite{fang2026hadamard}, with the relations of its Figure~6.
\begin{agdacode}[escapechar=@]
presentation : {n : ℕ} → (n VRel,_===_) IsSubPresentationOf U (2 ^ n)@\agdatag{agda:stab}@
presentation : {n : ℕ} → (_===_ {n}) IsPresentationOf (O n)@\agdatag{agda:hpi}@
\end{agdacode}

Agda checks every proof, so a reader only needs to check that each theorem statement matches its paper's result. Since every theorem is one of these two records, the reader checks only four components: the relations transcribed from the paper, the target group, the matrix of each gate, and the line assigning $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ to the record. All sixteen statements are collected in a single root module, so the reader can check them in one place.

\section{Formalization Workflow}\label{sec:method}

Our workflow has three parts. First, before formalizing a paper, we check its rewrite rules, its intermediate results, and each line of its derivations with exact matrices in short scripts. This caught most misprints before any Agda code was written. Second, we state each main theorem in QCert before a coding agent starts, so the agent only supplies the proof. Finally, for long derivations, the agent writes a script that turns each line of the paper into one step of the Agda proof, so a failing step points to a specific line of the paper. 
%With this workflow, coding agents wrote the fifteen formalizations other than qudit Clifford within four weeks, most in less than a day.

The main computational cost came from Agda's conversion checker. If two terms are syntactically identical (i.e., written in the same way), it accepts them immediately. Otherwise, it evaluates both, which can take exponential time for a long matrix product. For such products, we keep circuit interpretations abstract and decide matrix equality by computing a single Boolean using row operations, which is orders of magnitude faster than comparing matrices entry by entry. We record these techniques in the instructions followed by the agents, so later proofs avoid the same slowdowns.

\section{Corrections to the Source Papers}\label{sec:findings}

The formalizations in \Cref{tab:corpus} identify two kinds of issues in the source papers. Most are typos, and some of them appear in statements that the proofs rely on. These are easy to fix. The other two are harder to repair. One is a gap in a proof, and the other is a relation missing from a theorem. We describe both below.

The gap is in the proof of Theorem~4.14 of Hadamard-$\Pi$~\cite{fang2026hadamard}, which states that the relations in its Figure~6 present the group $O_n\left(\Z\left[1/\sqrt2\right]\right)$. One case of the proof relies on Lemmas~A.16 and~A.17, which have counterexamples in dimension~$6$, and the case analysis built on them misses a matrix in dimension~$9$. The authors have confirmed this gap (personal communication). Cl\'ement's unpublished notes~\cite{clement2019thesis} present the same group with different relations, and his case analysis covers the missing case. We prove that the two sets of relations are equivalent in every dimension and formalize Cl\'ement's proof~\cite{qcert2026clement}. Hence, Theorem~4.14 holds, so does Cl\'ement's completeness result for real Clifford+CH circuits~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch} which builds on it.

The missing relation is in Theorem~5.2 of~\cite{amy2024toffoli}. It states that the relations in its Figures~4 and~7, over the Toffoli and $\mathrm{K} = \mathrm{H}\otimes\mathrm{H}$ gates, present the group $O_8\left(\Z\left[1/2\right]\right)$. This is the first of two steps towards presenting the scaled dyadic orthogonal group $L_8$ by three-qubit Toffoli--Hadamard circuits. The relation $r_{\mathrm{K}}$ in Section~5.5, $\mathrm{K}_{1,2} = \mathrm{K}_{[4,5,6,7]}\,\mathrm{X}_0\,\mathrm{K}_{[4,5,6,7]}\,\mathrm{X}_0$ (see \cite{amy2024toffoli} for more details), is in neither figure. Nor can it be derived from them. Hence, Theorem~5.2 fails as stated, and we have informed the authors. Our formalization~\cite{qcert2026toffoli} shows that $r_{\mathrm{K}}$ is independent by giving an interpretation of circuits that every other relation preserves except $r_{\mathrm{K}}$, and proves the presentations of $O_8\left(\Z\left[1/2\right]\right)$ and $L_8$ with $r_{\mathrm{K}}$ restored.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We formalize the results of sixteen papers in QCert, using consistent circuit syntax, a unified definition of group presentations, and shared libraries for rings and their matrix groups. Since all formalizations use these definitions, presentations of the same group from different papers can be compared and proved equivalent. The same libraries, together with the induction that turns an exact synthesis algorithm into a completeness proof, can be reused for new algorithms. Only the case analysis over matrices and generators is specific to each algorithm. As the most error-prone part of the proof, it is where machine checking in QCert matters most.

At the moment, not every completeness result fits into QCert, as its scope is limited in two ways. First, it supports only discrete gate sets, so it does not cover completeness results for circuits with continuous parameters, such as rotations by arbitrary angles~\cite{clement2023complete,clement2024extensions,clement2024minimal}. Second, control is not a circuit constructor in QCert, so it does not cover results that treat control as a primitive operation on circuits~\cite{delorme2026control,schober2026control,blake2026qudit}.

We plan to extend the infrastructure of QCert to more families of quantum circuits, including the two above. Completeness is still open for many discrete gate sets in higher-dimensional quantum systems, and their case analyses are often too large to check by hand. In particular, we plan to study the completeness of Clifford circuits in all dimensions with the help of QCert. Beyond completeness, the exact synthesis libraries can be reused for T-count optimization of multi-qubit Clifford+T circuits. Finally, we are translating the formalizations from Agda into Lean, to make them available to its larger community. This will also allow us to take advantage of Lean's rich mathematical library Mathlib.
