\section{Introduction}
Rewriting of quantum circuits is a ubiquitous tool in circuit optimization~\cite{hietala2021voqc,nam2018automated,maslov2008simplification,xu2022quartz}, equivalence checking~\cite{yamashita2010fast}, hardware mapping~\cite{sivarajah2020tket}, and reasoning about quantum programs~\cite{fang2026hadamard}. This relies on identifying a set of rewrite rules for a family of quantum circuits that transform a circuit into an equivalent one.
Such a theory should be \emph{sound} because every rewrite preserves the underlying linear map. It should also be \emph{complete} so that any equivalence between circuits can be derived. Additionally, we want it to be \emph{minimal}, with no redundant rules. 
% Complete theories also support reasoning about quantum programs. For example,
% \mbox{Hadamard-$\Pi$}~\cite{fang2026hadamard} is a complete
% equational theory for reversible quantum programs. 

Completeness results have been established for a wide range of circuit families, including reversible linear circuits~\cite{lafont2003boolean,sakashita2013cnot}, reversible circuits over multiple-control Toffoli gates~\cite{feng2026complete,feng2026ancillas}, Clifford~\cite{selinger2015clifford,makary2021real,li2025qutrit,bian2026qupit}, CNOT-dihedral~\cite{amy2018cnotdihedral,blake2026phaseaffine}, two-qubit Clifford+T~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2}, three-qubit Clifford+CS~\cite{bian2023cliffordcs3}, Toffoli--Hadamard~\cite{amy2024toffoli} and real Clifford+CH~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch} circuits, as well as universal quantum circuits~\cite{clement2023complete,clement2024extensions}. Completeness has also been obtained for the group of two-level matrices underlying several of these circuit families~\cite{greylyn2014,bian2021un,li2021on,fang2026hadamard,clement2019thesis}. Minimal theories have been given for six of these circuit fragments~\cite{blake2026simpler} and for all quantum circuits~\cite{clement2024minimal}.

Completeness and minimality proofs typically require long case distinctions over gates, relations, and normal forms, whose supporting derivations sometimes span hundreds of pages~\cite{fang2026hadamard,li2025qutrit,clement2026realcliffordch}. In particular, relation reductions require intricate circuit manipulations and repeated rewrites, making them prone to human mistakes. For example, circular reasoning can lead us to remove a rule whose derivation still relies on that rule, hidden in a chain of other derivations. Errors can then propagate through dependent derivations, invalidating the entire reduction and making the error source hard to trace by hand. 

The length of these proofs and their error-prone nature makes it desirable to formally verify their correctness using a proof assistant like Lean or Agda. This would have not been feasible in the past due to  the sheer amount of lemmas and lengthy rewrites in these papers. However, the recent gain in capability of LLMs for writing correct code for proof assistants brings this closer to reality. Anthropic, for example, reports that LLMs wrote a 13-million-line Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem within 11 days~\cite{anthropic2026flt}. However, care must still be taken. It is important to manually verify the \emph{alignment} between the machine-verified statement and the natural language result that we wish to verify. For instance, in~\cite{bastounis2026navier}, it was shown that a claimed verification of a natural language proof actually contained mistranslations in the verification, and hence that the formal verification by the LLM was verifying a different proof for the same result. A way to solve this is to use a hybrid approach, where humans write and carefully review the definitions and theorem statements~\cite{hales2026reliability}, coding agents write the proofs, and Agda checks them.

\paragraph{Our contributions.} In this work we provide a human-constructed Agda~\cite{norell2007thesis,bove2009agda} framework for representing and verifying quantum circuit completeness and minimality results and use it verify correctness of 16 completeness results. This builds on our formalization of qudit Clifford completeness~\cite{bian2026qupit}, where we built the framework~\cite{bian2027popl} and carried out the main verification steps by hand. Since all 16 completeness results use the same framework of quantum circuits, gates and matrices over a finitely-generated ring, this makes it possible to efficiently check the alignment of the formalised results with the natural-language statements from the original papers. Of these 16 results, we fully verify 14 directly, and find fixes to verify the remaining 2. For one result we find a gap in one of the proofs that can be fixed, and for which the original completeness claim still holds. For one other result we find that the relation table is missing one rewrite, and we show that it is in fact independent of the other rules, so that completeness is not possible without it. 

\paragraph{Background}
A circuit is a word over an alphabet~$\mathcal{G}$ of gates. A rewrite rule is then a relation between words over this alphabet, and a rule set $\mathcal{R}$ generates a congruence over the gates. To a circuit $C$ we associate an \emph{interpretation} $\llbracket C \rrbracket$, which is its underlying matrix. The completeness results we formalise have matrix entries in the finitely-generated rings $\Z\left[1/\sqrt2,i\right]$, $\Z\left[1/2,i\right]$, $\Z\left[1/\sqrt2\right]$, and $\Z\left[1/2\right]$, which make them exactly representable in Agda. We leave the formalisation of universal circuits taking values in $\mathbb{C}$ for future work.

There are two main strategies for proving completeness of a rule set $\mathcal{R}$ over a gate set $\mathcal{G}$, both of which we support. Consider any two circuits $C_1$ and $C_2$ over $\mathcal{G}$ that have the same linear map, $\llbracket C_1\rrbracket = \llbracket C_2 \rrbracket$. We need to show that $C_1$ can be rewritten into $C_2$ using $\mathcal{R}$. \Cref{fig:normalform} outlines the first strategy. It specifies a unique normal form for each linear map~\cite{lafont2003boolean,sakashita2013cnot,feng2026complete,feng2026ancillas,selinger2015clifford,bian2026qupit,makary2021real,amy2018cnotdihedral,blake2026phaseaffine,li2025qutrit}, often by giving an exact synthesis algorithm~\cite{greylyn2014,bian2021un,li2021on,fang2026hadamard,clement2019thesis}. Completeness follows by proving that every circuit can be transformed into its normal form using the rewrite rules. By uniqueness, $C_1$ and $C_2$ have the same normal form $N$. Reversing the path from $C_2$ to $N$ and concatenating it with the path from $C_1$ to $N$ then gives the desired path of rewrites.

% C_1\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}N\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}C_2.
% \]

\Cref{fig:translation} illustrates the second strategy, which uses an already complete rule set $\mathcal{R}'$ over a different gate set $\mathcal{G}'$~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2,bian2023cliffordcs3,clement2026realcliffordch,amy2024toffoli,blake2026simpler,clement2023complete,clement2024extensions,delorme2026control,schober2026control,blake2026qudit}. Here, $E$ and $D$ translate circuits from $\mathcal{G}$ to $\mathcal{G}'$ and back, replacing each gate with a circuit over the other gate set. Both $E$ and $D$ preserve circuit composition and the underlying linear map. To transfer completeness from $\mathcal{R}'$ to $\mathcal{R}$, we first show that every rule of $\mathcal{R}'$, translated by $D$, can be derived using $\mathcal{R}$. We can then replace each step of the proof between $C_1'$ and $C_2'$ by its translated derivation, giving $D(C_1')\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_2')$. 
It then remains to prove that the translation $C_j\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_j')$ is provable within $\mathcal{R}$, for which it suffices to prove the round-trip translations $g\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(E(g))$ for each $g\in\mathcal{G}$.
% We also need to check that each gate can be rewritten to its round-trip translation: . Applying these rewrites gate by gate gives $C_j\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_j')$ for $j\in\{1,2\}$. Combining these paths yields
% \[
% C_1\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_1')\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}D(C_2')\rewrites{\mathcal{R}}C_2.
% \]
Sometimes the two strategies are combined, as in the scaled dyadic extension of~\cite{li2021on}.
 
Starting from a complete rule set, we can remove a redundant rule by deriving it from the other rules, thereby preserving completeness. To show that the resulting rule set is minimal, we need to give, for each remaining rule $R$, an interpretation of circuits that every other rule preserves but $R$ does not. Any derivation using the other rules preserves this interpretation, so $R$ cannot be derived from them~\cite{clement2024minimal,blake2026simpler}.

\section{The QCert Framework}
\label{sec:form}

Our Agda framework QCert states every completeness theorem as a group presentation. A circuit is a word over an alphabet~$X$ of gates, and the rules form a relation~$\Gamma$ on words, which generates a congruence $\approx$. 
Unlike the letters of an ordinary word, gates act on wires. The gates in $X$ are indexed by the number of wires they act on. Wires are numbered from $0$ at the bottom, and a gate always acts on the bottom wires of a circuit. To place it anywhere else, we apply a shift, which takes a circuit on $n$ wires and places it on wires $1,\dots,n$ of a circuit with $n+1$ wires. Each relation is stated on as many wires as the paper draws it on. To apply it inside a circuit with more wires, we need \emph{structural rules}, which hold for every circuit, regardless of its gate set. These rules say that an equation can be shifted up by one wire, gates on disjoint wires commute, and a scalar can be placed on any wire. These define the equations of a PROP~\cite{MacLane1965categorical}, and are often defined implicitly in completeness papers, but a formalization in Agda must define them explicitly.

We associate to circuits a matrix semantics $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ with entries in a finitely-generated ring like $\Z\left[1/\sqrt2,i\right]$. Hence, $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ defines a group homomorphism to a unitary matrix group $G$. We build $G$ from the group structures of Agda's standard library, which is checked only once per ring. As matrices, a gate on the bottom wires and a shift are Kronecker products with an identity, so the extension of $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ from gates to circuits is defined once for all gate sets. Since the structural rules are universal, their soundness is also proved only once. Then for each paper, it suffices to check the soundness of its own rules.
Soundness and completeness say the relations present the image of $\llbracket - \rrbracket$, which is the subgroup of $G$ generated by the gates. In Agda, mathematical structures such as groups are defined as records.
% Our earlier library~\cite{bian2027popl} expresses this as a record.
The record $\Gamma$~\aid{IsSubPresentationOf}~$G$ contains the semantics $\llbracket - \rrbracket$, with proofs of soundness and completeness. The record $\Gamma$~\aid{IsPresentationOf}~$G$ adds surjectivity, so the relations present all of $G$.

The formalization of each paper ends with its main theorem stated as a record of one of these two kinds, \aid{IsSubPresentationOf} or \aid{IsPresentationOf}. For example, \Cref{agda:stab} is the theorem for the real stabilizer circuits of~\cite{makary2021real}, with the relations R1--R16 of its Figure~1 lifted to $n$ wires. \Cref{agda:hpi} is the theorem for the two-level matrices of Hadamard-$\Pi$~\cite{fang2026hadamard}, with the relations of its Figure~6.
\begin{agdacode}[escapechar=@]
presentation : {n : ℕ} → (n VRel,_===_) IsSubPresentationOf U (2 ^ n)@\agdatag{agda:stab}@
presentation : {n : ℕ} → (_===_ {n}) IsPresentationOf (O n)@\agdatag{agda:hpi}@
\end{agdacode}

Agda checks every proof, so a reader only needs to check that each theorem statement matches its paper's result. Since every theorem is one of these two records, it suffices for a reader to check only four components: the relations transcribed from the paper, the target group, the matrix of each gate, and the line assigning $\llbracket - \rrbracket$ to the record. All sixteen statements are collected in a single root module, so the reader can check them in one place.

\section{Formalization Workflow}\label{sec:method}

Our workflow has three parts. First, before formalizing a paper, we check its rewrite rules, its intermediate results, and each line of its derivations with exact matrices in short scripts. This caught most typos before any Agda code was written. Second, we state each main theorem in QCert before a coding agent starts, so the agent only supplies the proof. Finally, for long derivations, the agent writes a script that turns each line of the paper into one step of the Agda proof, so a failing step points to a specific line of the paper. 
%With this workflow, coding agents wrote the fifteen formalizations other than qudit Clifford within four weeks, most in less than a day.

The main computational cost came from Agda's conversion checker. If two terms are syntactically identical (i.e., written in the same way), it accepts them immediately. Otherwise, it evaluates both, which can take exponential time for a long matrix product. For such products, we keep circuit interpretations abstract and decide matrix equality by computing a single Boolean using row operations, which is orders of magnitude faster than comparing matrices entry by entry. We record these techniques in the instructions followed by the agents, so later proofs avoid the same slowdowns.

\section{Corrections to the Source Papers}\label{sec:findings}

The formalizations in \Cref{tab:corpus} identify two kinds of issues in the source papers. Most are typos, and some of them appear in statements that the proofs rely on. These are easy to fix. Two others are harder to repair. One is a gap in a proof, and the other is a relation missing from a theorem. We describe both below.

The gap is in the proof of Theorem~4.14 of Hadamard-$\Pi$~\cite{fang2026hadamard}, which states that the relations in its Figure~6 present the group $O_n\left(\Z\left[1/\sqrt2\right]\right)$. One case of the proof relies on Lemmas~A.16 and~A.17, which have counterexamples in dimension~$6$, and the case analysis built on them misses a matrix in dimension~$9$. The authors have confirmed this gap (personal communication). 
Their main result is however still correct: Cl\'ement's unpublished notes~\cite{clement2019thesis} present the same group with different relations, and his case analysis covers the missing case. We then prove that the two sets of relations are equivalent in every dimension and formalize Cl\'ement's proof~\cite{qcert2026clement}. Hence, the original Theorem~4.14 still holds and so does Cl\'ement's completeness result for real Clifford+CH circuits~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch} which builds on it.

The missing relation is in Theorem~5.2 of~\cite{amy2024toffoli}. It states that the relations in its Figures~4 and~7, over the Toffoli and $\mathrm{K} = \mathrm{H}\otimes\mathrm{H}$ gates, present the group $O_8\left(\Z\left[1/2\right]\right)$. This is the first of two steps towards presenting the scaled dyadic orthogonal group $L_8$ by three-qubit Toffoli--Hadamard circuits. The relation $r_{\mathrm{K}}$ in Section~5.5, $\mathrm{K}_{1,2} = \mathrm{K}_{[4,5,6,7]}\,\mathrm{X}_0\,\mathrm{K}_{[4,5,6,7]}\,\mathrm{X}_0$, is in neither figure. Nor can it be derived from them. Hence, Theorem~5.2 fails as stated, and we have informed the authors. Our formalization~\cite{qcert2026toffoli} shows that $r_{\mathrm{K}}$ is independent by giving an interpretation of circuits that every other relation preserves except $r_{\mathrm{K}}$, and proves the presentations of $O_8\left(\Z\left[1/2\right]\right)$ and $L_8$ with $r_{\mathrm{K}}$ restored.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We formalize the results of sixteen papers in QCert, using consistent circuit syntax, a unified definition of group presentations, and shared libraries for rings and their matrix groups. Since all formalizations use these definitions, presentations of the same group from different papers can be compared and proved equivalent. The same libraries, together with the induction that turns an exact synthesis algorithm into a completeness proof, can be reused for new algorithms. Only the case analysis over matrices and generators is specific to each algorithm. As the most error-prone part of the proof, it is where machine checking in QCert matters most.

At the moment, not every completeness result fits into QCert, as its scope is limited in two ways. First, it supports only discrete gate sets, so it does not cover completeness results for circuits with continuous parameters, such as rotations by arbitrary angles~\cite{clement2023complete,clement2024extensions,clement2024minimal}. Second, control is not a circuit constructor in QCert, so it does not cover results that treat control as a primitive operation on circuits~\cite{delorme2026control,schober2026control,blake2026qudit}.

We plan to extend the infrastructure of QCert to more families of quantum circuits, including the two above. Completeness is still open for many discrete gate sets in higher-dimensional quantum systems, and their case analyses are often too large to check by hand. In particular, we plan to study the completeness of Clifford circuits in all dimensions with the help of QCert. Beyond completeness, the exact synthesis libraries can be reused for T-count optimization of multi-qubit Clifford+T circuits. Finally, we are translating the formalizations from Agda into Lean, to make them available to its larger community. This will also allow us to take advantage of Lean's rich mathematical library Mathlib~\cite{mathlib2020}.
