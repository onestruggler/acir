\section{Introduction}
An equational theory for a family of quantum circuits is a set of equations between circuits used as rewrite rules. By applying these rules to local subcircuits, we can transform a circuit into an equivalent one. Such a theory should be \emph{sound} because every rewrite preserves the underlying linear map. It should also be \emph{complete} so that any equivalence between circuits can be derived. Additionally, we want it to be \emph{minimal}, with no redundant rules. Equational rewriting supports circuit optimization~\cite{hietala2021voqc,nam2018automated,maslov2008simplification,xu2022quartz}, equivalence checking~\cite{yamashita2010fast}, and hardware mapping~\cite{sivarajah2020tket}. Completeness ensures that every circuit equivalence can be derived within the theory, making it a powerful tool for reasoning about quantum programs. For example,
\mbox{Hadamard-$\Pi$}~\cite{fang2026hadamard} provides a complete
equational framework for reasoning about reversible quantum programs. Since 2014, completeness results have been established for a wide range of circuit families such as Clifford~\cite{selinger2015clifford,makary2021real,li2025qutrit,bian2026qupit}, CNOT-dihedral~\cite{amy2018cnotdihedral,blake2026phaseaffine}, two-qubit Clifford+T~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2}, three-qubit Clifford+CS~\cite{bian2023cliffordcs3}, Toffoli--Hadamard~\cite{amy2024toffoli} and real Clifford+CH~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch} circuits, and with continuous parameters, all quantum circuits~\cite{clement2023complete}. Completeness has also been obtained for the group of two-level matrices underlying several of these circuit families~\cite{greylyn2014,bian2021un,li2021on,fang2026hadamard,clement2019thesis}. Minimal theories have been given for six of these circuit fragments~\cite{blake2026simpler} and for all quantum circuits~\cite{clement2024minimal}.


Completeness proofs use two main strategies. \Cref{fig:normalform} outlines the first one. It specifies a unique normal form for each linear map~\cite{selinger2015clifford,bian2026qupit,makary2021real,amy2018cnotdihedral,blake2026phaseaffine,li2025qutrit}, often through giving an exact synthesis algorithm~\cite{greylyn2014,bian2021un,li2021on,fang2026hadamard,clement2019thesis}. Completeness follows by proving that every circuit can be transformed into its normal form using the rewrite rules. \Cref{fig:translation} illustrates the second strategy, which leverages the complete equational theory of another gate set. Completeness carries over if every rule of that theory, translated back, can be derived from the new rules. The new rules must also prove that each gate is equal to its round-trip translation~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2,bian2023cliffordcs3,clement2026realcliffordch,amy2024toffoli,blake2026simpler,clement2023complete}. Sometimes the two methods are combined, as in the scaled dyadic extension of~\citet{li2021on}. 
%It pairs a normal form with reduction to an already complete subgroup. 

Starting from a complete rule set, we can remove a redundant rule by deriving it from those other rules, thereby preserving completeness. To show that the resulting set is minimal, we need to give, for each remaining rule, an interpretation of circuits that every other rule preserves except that rule. Any derivation using the other rules preserves this interpretation, so the rule cannot be derived from them~\cite{clement2024minimal,blake2026simpler}.

Completeness and minimality proofs typically require long case distinctions over gates, relations and normal forms, whose supporting derivations sometimes run to hundreds of pages~\cite{fang2026hadamard,li2025qutrit,clement2026realcliffordch}. Long reductions of relation sets can also hide circular reasoning, where a derivation used to eliminate a relation depends indirectly on that same relation. Such dependencies are hard to detect by hand. An unnoticed gap can also undermine later proofs that rely on the result: Section~\ref{sec:findings} reports a gap in a published proof that a later theorem had imported. A machine check verifies the
individual cases and guards against circular arguments. To check
results that build on one another, it must be done in one framework,
which defines circuits, their semantics and completeness once, so that
a theorem proved in one development can be used in another.

Language models can now write large formal proofs. For example, a
13-million-line proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean was written in
11 days~\cite{anthropic2026flt}. Human review is still needed to check
that formal statements and their underlying definitions express the
intended results~\cite{hales2026reliability}. Generated proofs can be
too large to inspect in full. We therefore use a \emph{scaffold}: code
written and checked by people that fixes the form of each theorem.
This keeps each paper's checks short while Agda verifies the proofs.
The scaffold builds on an Agda library of circuits and presentations~\cite{bian2027popl}.

This work builds on our formalization of the completeness theorem for
qudit Clifford circuits~\cite{bian2026qupit}. There, we developed the
framework by hand and used LLM coding agents only at the final stage
to complete the proof. This experience motivated us to formalize
circuit completeness results within a common framework. We use this
framework to formalize sixteen papers (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}) and
make four contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item QCert extends an earlier library of words, circuits and
  presentations~\cite{bian2027popl} with matrix semantics over the
  rings of exact synthesis and a framework for proving independence
  of rules. Every theorem has the same form (Section~\ref{sec:form}).
\item We give machine-checked proofs of the sixteen theorems in about
  1{,}500 modules and 420{,}000 lines of Agda, with no postulates. The
  corpus typechecks in about 40 minutes (Section~\ref{sec:corpus}).
\item We identify and repair a gap in the completeness proof of
  Hadamard-$\Pi$, identify a rule omitted from a Toffoli--Hadamard
  theory, and report misprints in the other papers
  (Section~\ref{sec:findings}).
\item We present a method in which people develop the framework and
  state the theorems, LLM coding agents write the proofs, and Agda
  checks them (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\end{itemize}

\section{A Common Framework for Completeness Theorems}\label{sec:form}

Relations between words over an alphabet $X$ generate a congruence
$\approx$. A semantics is a monoid homomorphism
$\llbracket - \rrbracket$ from words to a monoid $M$. Soundness means
that related words have equal images; completeness means that words
with equal images are related. Together, these properties give a
presentation of the image of $\llbracket - \rrbracket$. If the map
is also surjective, the relations present $M$. For circuit families
with matrix semantics, $M$ is a matrix group, and the image is the
group generated by the gates.

The earlier library~\cite{bian2027popl} expresses a presentation
theorem as a record, $\Gamma$ \aid{IsSubPresentationOf} $G$. This
contains an interpretation of words in a group $G$ and proofs that the
induced map on words modulo $\approx$ is an injective group
homomorphism. The record $\Gamma$ \aid{IsPresentationOf} $G$ also
requires this map to be surjective.

A gate set is a family indexed by arity. A gate acts on the bottom
wires of a circuit, and a shift moves a circuit up by one wire.
Relations stated at the width at which they are drawn are extended by
structural rules: equations can be shifted up by one wire, gates on
disjoint wires commute, and scalars can be placed on any wire. The
library proves these rules sound once for all gate sets. We add matrix
semantics over the rings of exact synthesis,
$\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2},i]$, $\Z[\frac12,i]$,
$\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$ and $\Z[\frac12]$, with their unitary and
orthogonal groups expressed using the group structures of Agda's
standard library. Operators on bit vectors
and the Kronecker calculus allow us to define the matrix interpretation
of circuits once for all gate sets.

Every development concludes with a theorem of the same form. For
example, the real stabilizer circuits of~\citet{makary2021real}, with
the relations R1--R16 of their Figure~1 lifted to $n$ wires, give
\begin{agdacode}
presentation : {n : ℕ} → (n VRel,_===_) IsSubPresentationOf U (2 ^ n)
\end{agdacode}
For the two-level matrices of Hadamard-$\Pi$, with the relations of
its Figure~6, we obtain
\begin{agdacode}
presentation : {n : ℕ} → (_===_ {n}) IsPresentationOf (O n)
\end{agdacode}
A reader checks four components: the relations transcribed from the
paper, the target group, the matrix interpretation of each gate, and
the line assigning this interpretation to the record. The target groups
are checked once for all papers. For the real stabilizer development,
these checks involve fewer than 500 of its 12{,}010 lines. A single
root module states all sixteen main theorems in this form and provides
a starting point for reading the formalizations.

The proofs follow a few common methods. For \emph{exact synthesis}, an
algorithm expresses each matrix as a word in the generators.
Completeness follows by well-founded induction on a level, using a
Main Lemma that closes a square for every state and generator. We
formalize the induction once and prove the Main Lemma for each
presentation. For \emph{normal forms}, each circuit is rewritten into
a normal form recoverable from its matrix. For \emph{induction on the
width}, we decompose a circuit into a circuit on one fewer wire and a
component on the bottom wire. For \emph{encoding}, we translate
circuits into a complete theory and back.

Two developments reuse Agda proofs by Bian and
Selinger~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2,bian2023cliffordcs3}. These relate
circuit relations to two-level relations using the
Reidemeister--Schreier method. We connect them to our two-level
presentations to obtain statements about matrices. The qudit Clifford theorem
of~\citet{bian2026qupit} uses an algebraic model of the Clifford group,
built from the Pauli and symplectic groups together with a phase. The
other theorems use matrices.

Much of each development is generated. Computational derivations,
such as those in Selinger's 105-page supplement or the qutrit box
relations, are generated by scripts that follow the paper or search
for the steps. Agda checks these derivations using a rewriter and
decides soundness by evaluating matrices. Conceptual arguments, such
as the Main Lemma or uniqueness of a normal form, are formalized
directly. In the real stabilizer development, half the lines are
generated and a fifth formalize the paper's mathematics.

\section{Findings from the Formalizations}\label{sec:findings}

\paragraph{A gap in Hadamard-$\Pi$.} Theorem~4.14 of Hadamard-$\Pi$
states that the relations in its Figure~6 present the group
$O_n(\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}])$ of orthogonal matrices over
$\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$. The proof uses exact synthesis. One subcase of
its Main Lemma relies on Lemmas~A.16 and~A.17, both of which are
false. Each fails on a unit column in dimension~6, such as
$(-1-\sqrt2, 1, -1-\sqrt2, 1, \sqrt2, -2+\sqrt2)/\sqrt2^4$.
The underlying calculation omits a $+1$ from an expression that is
reduced modulo~2.
By testing the case analysis on random states, we also found a state
in dimension~9 for which every diagram provided in the proof exceeds
the required level bound. The proof of the Main Lemma therefore does
not cover this state.

The theorem itself still holds. Cl\'ement's unpublished
2019 thesis~\cite{clement2019thesis} presents the same group using a
different set of relations, and its case analysis covers the missing
case. We prove that the two sets of relations are equivalent in every
dimension. We then formalize Cl\'ement's case analysis as a decision
tree checked by evaluation in Agda, together with the rest of his
proof. Cl\'ement's 2026 theorem on real Clifford+CH
circuits~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch} uses Theorem~4.14 and thus
depends on the same gap. Our formalization supplies the missing proof
of Theorem~4.14.

\paragraph{An omitted relation.} Amy, Ross and
Wesley~\cite{amy2024toffoli} present three-qubit Toffoli--Hadamard
circuits in two steps. They first present $O_8(\Z[\frac12])$ using
the Toffoli and $\mathrm{K} = \mathrm{H}\otimes\mathrm{H}$ gates,
and then present the scaled dyadic orthogonal group $L_8$.
Their Section~5.5 uses a relation $r_{\mathrm{K}}$ that defines
$\mathrm{K}_{1,2}$ in terms of a controlled K gate. However, this
relation is omitted from the figures specifying the relations of
Theorem~5.2, and it cannot be derived from the remaining relations.
To show this, we interpret each Toffoli--K gate $g$ as $RgR$, where
$R = I - J/4$ and $J$ is the all-ones matrix. This interpretation
satisfies every other relation but fails to satisfy $r_{\mathrm{K}}$.
Thus the relations as printed are incomplete. After restoring
$r_{\mathrm{K}}$, we prove that they present $L_8$.

\paragraph{Misprints.} The other papers are correct in the parts we
formalize, up to misprints, several of which occur in statements used
by the proofs. These include eleven of the 137 typed relations
of~\citet{makary2021real}, one relation in the $L_n$ table
of~\citet{li2021on}, the scalar of a qutrit rule
in~\citet{blake2026simpler}, two equations in the encoding
of~\citet{clement2026realcliffordch}, five formulas
of~\citet{amy2024toffoli}, and one box relation
of~\citet{li2025qutrit}. In each case, either the statement failed a
numerical check or a derivation failed at the step that used it.

\section{Formalization Workflow}\label{sec:method}

LLM coding agents wrote the fifteen developments other than qudit
Clifford under our direction within four weeks. Most took less than a
day each. Our workflow has three parts. First, we check claims using
exact matrices in short scripts before formalizing them. This caught
most misprints before any Agda code was written. Second, scripts
generate long derivations following the paper, so a failing step can
be traced to a specific line. Finally, the framework fixes the
relation, group and presentation record, leaving the agent to supply
the proof.

The main computational cost came from Agda's conversion checker,
which compares terms by unfolding their definitions. For long matrix
products, comparing syntactically different expressions can take
exponential time. We therefore keep circuit interpretations
abstract and decide matrix equality by computing a single Boolean
using row operations. This is a hundred to four hundred times faster
than comparing matrices entry by entry. All proofs are constructive:
they use neither excluded middle nor choice, and do not require real
numbers. Each case split is a decision procedure, and each proof is a
program. Together with decidable matrix equality, soundness and
completeness give an executable decision procedure for each theory's
word problem.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We formalize sixteen developments within a common framework, with
shared circuit syntax, presentation records, and libraries for rings
and their matrix groups. This allows us to relate different
presentations, as in our proof that Cl\'ement's relations and those of
Hadamard-$\Pi$ are equivalent.

The framework provides a foundation for completeness in
higher-dimensional systems, where case analyses can become difficult
to check by hand. The qutrit theorem, for example, relies on hundreds
of box relations. We decide soundness by evaluating exact matrices,
generate and check derivations in Agda, and typecheck the corpus in
about 40 minutes. The rings, matrix groups and induction from a Main
Lemma to completeness (\Cref{sec:corpus}) also support
number-theoretic exact synthesis. These components are formalized once
and can be reused for new algorithms. Each algorithm still requires a
proof of its Main Lemma, where the gap in Hadamard-$\Pi$ occurs
(\Cref{sec:findings}).

\paragraph{Future work.} We are porting the developments to Lean to
make our theorems available to its larger community and use them with
its broader mathematical library, Mathlib. We also plan to study
Clifford completeness in all dimensions and T-count optimization for
multi-qubit Clifford+T exact synthesis.
