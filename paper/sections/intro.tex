\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Equational reasoning is the daily bread of circuit design.  Whether one
optimizes a quantum circuit, verifies a compiler pass, or normalizes a
diagram, the justification is always the same: a finite set of local rewrite
rules, applied to a syntactic object, preserving a semantic interpretation.
Two questions attach to any such rule set.  \emph{Soundness} (does every
rule preserve the semantics?) is typically easy: it is a finite collection
of small algebraic identities.  \emph{Completeness} (can every true
semantic equation be derived from the rules?) is a different animal
altogether.

Completeness proofs are gigantic computations.  The standard strategy defines
a \emph{normal form} for circuits, proves that every circuit can be rewritten
to normal form using the rules, and proves that distinct normal forms denote
distinct semantic objects.  The middle step is where the explosion happens:
one must show, for every rule and every way a generator can meet a normal
form, that the combination reduces again, a case analysis that is
mechanical, unforgiving, and enormous.  The literature wears the scars
openly.  Selinger's presentation of the $n$-qubit Clifford group already
required a substantial rewrite system and normal-form
analysis~\cite{selinger2015clifford}.  Its recent qutrit
analogue by Li, Mosca, Ross, van~de~Wetering, and Zhao, the first
completeness result for a gate set of quantum circuits in an odd prime
dimension, runs to more than fifty pages, with a further supplement of
167 pages of derivations~\cite{li2025qutrit}.  The first complete equational theory for full
quantum circuits, by Cl\'ement, Heurtel, Mansfield, Perdrix, and
Valiron~\cite{clement2023complete}, and its minimal
refinement~\cite{clement2024minimal}, rest on intricate normal-form
manipulations; and Cl\'ement's complete equational theory for
Real-Clifford+$CH$ circuits consists of a thirteen-page paper attached to an
appendix of \emph{264 pages} of equational
derivations~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch}.  These are not outliers:
tens of pages of mechanical calculation also stand behind the
rig-categorical completeness theorems for quantum programming
languages~\cite{carette2024squareroots,fang2026hadamard} and behind the
recent uniform simplification of the rule sets of six near-Clifford circuit
fragments~\cite{blake2026simpler}.  These are long, hand-written,
hand-checked derivations, every one of which deserves a machine's
scrutiny.  Proofs of this shape, long, regular, and computational, are
precisely the proofs that should be machine-checked, and precisely the
proofs that a proof assistant with a strong evaluator can check largely
\emph{by computation}.

This paper reports on an Agda library built for exactly this purpose: a
general, reusable formal framework for \emph{presentations of monoids and
groups by generators and relations}, for \emph{normal forms}, and for
\emph{completeness} of finite relation sets, with circuits (words of
wire-indexed gates) as the motivating instance.  In plain terms, a circuit
is a word of gates, a rule set is a datatype of axioms, and a normal form
is a function on words with a section back; completeness then follows
from soundness and the uniqueness of normal forms, and
Section~\ref{sec:permutations} shows the whole route on permutation
circuits.  The library is written in
safe, axiom-free Agda~\cite{norell2007thesis,bove2009agda} on top of the
Agda standard library~\cite{daggitt2025stdlib,agdastdlib}, and is being
prepared for submission to it; the semantic constructions it needed and
the standard library lacked (semidirect products, amalgamated free products
of monoids, group extensions, epimorphisms, the permutation group bundled as
a group) are staged in a dedicated \texttt{ForStdlib} hierarchy.

One design decision deserves to be announced up front: a presented
monoid is a setoid on words, not a quotient type, so the library is
written in plain Agda rather than in cubical Agda or a HoTT library.  The
reason is that the library computes with syntax: coset tables and
normal-form maps pattern-match on concrete words, and its central devices
are maps \emph{into} the syntax (sections of normal forms, coset
representatives, amalgamation tails), which need concrete
representatives to land on; \S\ref{sec:setoids} weighs the
trade.

\paragraph{Why this is a programming-languages problem.}
Completeness of a circuit calculus is a program\-ming-lan\-guages question:
it licenses normal-form decision procedures, tells an optimizer that no
valid rewrite is missing, and underlies the rig-categorical completeness
theorems of recent
POPLs~\cite{choudhury2022symmetries,carette2024squareroots,fang2026hadamard}.
Our contribution is a \emph{design}: presentations, normal forms, and
completeness organized in a dependently typed language so that the same
objects both compute and prove, validated on examples large enough to
exercise every part of it.

\subsection{Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(0)] \textbf{A library for circuit normal forms and complete relation
  sets.}  An Agda library in which a relation set is an inductive
  family on words, the presented monoid is the inductively generated
  congruence closure (\S\ref{sec:presentations}), normal-form witnesses are the
  standard library's function bundles out of the word setoid, and a single
  generic lemma, \aid{by-normalization}, turns soundness plus a \emph{unique
  normal form} into completeness (\S\ref{sec:nfproperty}).  A verified,
  monoid-level Reidemeister--Schreier engine constructs normal forms from
  verified coset tables, one per level
  (\S\ref{sec:engine}), and solvers close associativity and
  normal-form goals by evaluation (\S\ref{sec:solvers}).

\item[(1)] \textbf{Inductively defined circuits, for easy normalization and
  completeness proofs.}  A generic circuit layer represents $n$-wire circuits
  as words over wire-indexed generators, and extends any gate-specific
  relation set with the structural rules shared by every circuit presentation
  (\S\ref{sec:circuits}).  The indexing discipline is chosen so that
  coset tables can be written by direct pattern matching and, where their
  obligations reduce to computation, checked by Agda's evaluator.

\item[(2)] \textbf{A catalogue of verified presentations}
  (\S\ref{sec:examples}): cyclic groups of every positive order (and
  $\mathbb{N}$ at order $0$); symmetric groups $S_n$ presented as circuits,
  complete for an endofunction semantics and presented
  for the group of permutations of $\mathsf{Fin}\;n$; wreath products
  $\mathbb{Z}_N \wr S_n$, the generalized symmetric groups (signed
  permutations at $N = 2$);
  Pauli groups modulo phases, $(\mathbb{Z}_p \times \mathbb{Z}_p)^n$
  for every prime $p$, assembled purely compositionally; and, as
  amalgamated free products with verified alternating
  normal forms, the presentations of the single-qubit and single-qutrit
  Clifford+$T$ monoids and of $U_3(\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12,i])$.  Every headline theorem is
  restated, with its full type, in a single index module
  \texttt{MainTheorems.agda}.

\item[(3)] \textbf{Monoid-level amalgamation and Reidemeister--Schreier, as
  reusable modules.}
  Following the methodology that Bian and Selinger introduced for their
  syntactic verifications~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2,bian2023cliffordcs3,
  bian2023thesis}, we work with the monoid-level versions of the
  group-theoretic notions (coset tables, the Reidemeister--Schreier
  subgroup presentation, and amalgamated free products), where inverses
  are not primitive, and package them as generic modules with explicit
  hypothesis records (\S\ref{sec:engine}, \S\ref{sec:constructions}).

\item[(4)] \textbf{Presentation theorems for the product constructions.}
  Beyond the syntactic side, we prove the
  corresponding \emph{semantic} presentation theorems: if $\Gamma$ presents
  $G_1$ and $\Delta$ presents $G_2$, the constructed relation sets present
  the direct product, the semidirect product, the $n$-fold product, the
  amalgamated free product, and (given a realizing short exact sequence) an
  arbitrary group extension (\S\ref{sec:constructions}).

\item[(5)] \textbf{New constructions and theorems for the standard library}:
  semidirect products of monoids and groups via a bundled
  \aid{Action} record; amalgamated free products of monoids as a pushout;
  group extensions
  and split extensions as short exact sequences; monoid epimorphisms; the
  fact that a monoid homomorphism between groups is a group homomorphism;
  and the group of permutations of $\mathsf{Fin}\;n$ bundled as a
  \aid{Group} (\S\ref{sec:forstdlib}).
\end{enumerate}

Everything stated as a theorem in this paper typechecks with the flags
\texttt{-{}-cubical-compatible} and \texttt{-{}-safe}: there are no
postulates, no unfinished holes, and no appeals to classical axioms
anywhere in the development described here.  The development is also
constructive in a way that matters: normal forms and their sections
compute, so every completeness theorem doubles as a decision procedure
for the word problem of its theory.  Wherever a statement in this
paper is \emph{not} formalized, the text says so explicitly, and
\S\ref{sec:threats} discusses the main such gap.

\subsection{Paper outline}

Section~\ref{sec:permutations} walks through the permutation case study.
Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} recalls the group theory behind the design:
normal forms from subgroup chains and transversals, and their mixed-radix
reading.  Section~\ref{sec:design} presents the library layer by layer:
words and presentations, the zoo of normal-form witnesses, inductively
defined circuits, the Reidemeister--Schreier engine, the product
constructions with their presentation theorems, the semantic constructions
contributed to the standard library, and the solvers.  Section~\ref{sec:examples}
is the catalogue of verified results, with pointers into
\texttt{MainTheorems.agda}.  Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys related work
and compares with other formalizations.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses design decisions, statistics, and lessons learned, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
