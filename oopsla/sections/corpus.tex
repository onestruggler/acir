\emph{presentation} means that the relations present the whole matrix

\subsection{Two-level matrix groups}

Four of the sources present groups of two-level matrices by exact
synthesis, and the fifth (Hadamard-$\Pi$) does too. We formalized each
of them with the same skeleton, the levels and edges of
Section~\ref{sec:levels}.

Greylyn's thesis~\cite{greylyn2014} presents $U_4(\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2},i])$,
the group of two-qubit Clifford+$T$ operators; our development states
the relations for every $n$ and proves completeness for $n\le4$, as the
thesis does. Bian and Selinger~\cite{bian2021un} present
$U_n(\Z[\frac12,i])$, the group behind Clifford+$CS$, for every $n$.
Li, Ross and Selinger~\cite{li2021on} present $O_n(\Z[\frac12])$ with the
generators $(-1)_{[a]}$, $X_{[a,b]}$ and $K_{[a,b,c,d]}=H\otimes H$, and,
in their Section~5, the group $L_n$ of scaled dyadic orthogonal
matrices, which adds $I\otimes H$; we formalized both, with normal
syllables that carry one sign where theirs carry four
(Section~\ref{sec:beyond}). Hadamard-$\Pi$~\cite{fang2026hadamard}
presents $O_n(\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}])$ by the generators $Z_{[a]}$,
$X_{[a,b]}$ and $H_{[a,b]}$ and the relations (a1)--(d4) of its
Figure~6; we formalized generation, Algorithm~1 and its correctness,
soundness, and the Main Lemma up to the one case that the paper's proof
does not cover, which the theorem takes as a hypothesis. Cl\'ement's
thesis of 2019~\cite{clement2019thesis} presents the same group with
relations (1)--(21); we proved that the two relation sets generate the
same congruence in every dimension, proved the missing case after his
Case~3.4, and then formalized his whole proof over his own version of
the algorithm. Section~\ref{sec:gap} tells this story.

\subsection{Circuits}

Selinger's Clifford circuits~\cite{selinger2015clifford} and Makary,
Ross and Selinger's real stabilizer circuits~\cite{makary2021real} are
proved complete by normal forms, with supplements of derivations that
we followed line by line (Section~\ref{sec:search}). Both papers count
their normal forms, and so the order of the group,
$8\prod_{i=1}^n 2(4^i-1)4^i$ for Clifford and
$2\prod_{i=1}^n (4^i+2^i-2)(2\cdot4^{i-1})$ for real stabilizers. We
proved the counts as bijections between the normal forms and
$\mathsf{Fin}$ of the order, and, for the real case, that every
orthogonal matrix that normalizes the Pauli group is the matrix of a
circuit, over any ring whose only square roots of $1$ are $\pm1$.

Amy, Chen and Ross~\cite{amy2018cnotdihedral} present the CNOT-dihedral
operators, generated by $\omega$, $X$, $T$ and $CNOT$, and Blake's
phase-affine circuits~\cite{blake2026phaseaffine} form four levels in
each prime dimension $d$: affine circuits, then phases, then $S$, then
$T$, the last two for odd $d$ and $d>3$. Both proofs go by induction on
the number of wires (Section~\ref{sec:width}). The proof
of~\cite{amy2018cnotdihedral} builds on known presentations of linear
reversible circuits; our development proves that part too, by a coset
tower in the style of Lafont~\cite{lafont2003boolean}.

Bian and Selinger's papers on two-qubit Clifford+$T$ and three-qubit
Clifford+$CS$ circuits~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2,bian2023cliffordcs3}
came with Agda code, which proves only that the circuit relations
present the same group as a set of two-level relations, along a
translation of generators, and does not mention matrices. We ported it
to our library and connected it to the two-level presentations above
at $n=4$ and $n=8$ (Section~\ref{sec:ports}), which gives the semantic
theorem: the circuit relations present the image of the circuits in
$U_4$ and $U_8$.

Cl\'ement's theory of real Clifford+$CH$
circuits~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch} is proved complete width by
width: by a normal form on one qubit, and on two qubits and more by
encoding circuits as words over two-level matrices, or over a group $P$
of pairs of them, and back, using completeness of those words (its
Theorem~4.4, imported from Hadamard-$\Pi$, and its Theorem~4.10, proved
by the Reidemeister--Schreier method). The paper numbers its equations
up to (361), most of them auxiliary equations for the decoding, proved
by induction on the width with completeness one width down as a black
box. All of it is formalized, and Section~\ref{sec:encoding} describes
how the imported theorem is discharged.

\subsection{Qudit Clifford circuits}\label{sec:qupit}

\citet{bian2026qupit} give sixteen rules, C0--C15 of their Figure~1,
for Clifford circuits on qudits of odd prime dimension $p$, over the
generators $-\omega$, $H$, $S$ and $CZ$, where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/p}$.
Their theorem was formalized in Agda alongside the paper, with the same
library, and we include that development here. Unlike the others, it
was written mainly by hand, with an LLM agent for parts of it; it is the
earliest of the fourteen developments and took the longest, about three
months.

The proof works first at the symplectic level, and the development goes
up in layers, each a presentation theorem. $Sp(2n,\Z_p)$ is presented by
a normal form computed by a coset tower in the style of Reidemeister
and Schreier, one level per wire, its uniqueness proved by induction on
the width; a simplified rule set, stated relative to a primitive root
$g$ of $\Z_p$, presents the same group. The semidirect product of the
Pauli group and $Sp(2n,\Z_p)$, which is the Clifford group modulo
scalars, is assembled by a generic construction from presentations of
the two factors and of the conjugation action
(Section~\ref{sec:products}). The circuits read modulo scalars present
this product under five rule sets over $H$, $S$ and $CZ$: the first is
where the mathematics is, the second simplifies its relations further,
the other three are spellings of Figure~1 without its scalars, and each
is related to one before it by an isomorphism of rule sets along which
the theorem transports. The scalars come back in two steps: a central
extension by $\langle\omega\rangle\cong\Z_p$ given by the Weyl cocycle,
whose rules are those below each multiplied by the power of $\omega$
it holds up to, which is trivial except for
$(SH)^3 = \omega^{(p^2-1)/8}$; and, since the scalars of that group
have odd order and it does not contain $-1$, a direct factor
$\langle -1\rangle$, the paper's group being generated by $-\omega$.
Figure~1 presents that product, through an isomorphism of rule sets
that reads $-\omega$ as $-1\cdot\omega$. The rule C4 can be stated with
the multiplier $M_g$ on either side of $S$; both versions are
formalized, and each is derived from the other.

The groups on the right of these theorems are algebraic models. An
element is a Pauli and a symplectic matrix over $\Z_p$, with a phase in
$\Z_p$ and a sign. That this model is the group of unitary matrices
that the circuits generate is the Weyl representation, which is
standard but not formalized. So unlike the other circuit developments,
this one does not say that the rules derive every equation between
circuits with the same unitary matrix. It says that they derive every
equation that holds in the model.

\subsection{Minimality and independence}\label{sec:structural}

Blake~\cite{blake2026simpler} gives smaller presentations for six
fragments of near-Clifford circuits and proves them minimal: no rule
follows from the others. The proofs use separating interpretations, a
model in which all rules but one hold. We formalized the six fragments
with their matrix semantics, soundness at every width, and all the
separating interpretations of the minimality theorem, on the
independence framework of Section~\ref{sec:independence-layer}. The
completeness part of that paper, a translation from the earlier
presentations, is not formalized.

We used the same framework on our own circuit layer. Its four
structural rules (Section~\ref{sec:presentations}) are independent: for
each rule there is a family of monoids, one per width, in which the
other three hold and that one fails. The models record how many times
each gate has been shifted up. A fifth rule that an earlier version of
this work had, that a scalar commutes with every gate, is derivable
from the four when scalars commute with each other. With two scalars it
is not, and a fifth model shows it.
