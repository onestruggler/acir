\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Formalizing mathematics has been slow. A non-trivial theorem could take
dozens or hundreds of person-hours to formalize, and a large one years
of work by a team. This has changed. In September 2026 a language model
wrote a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean, 13 million lines in 11
days~\cite{anthropic2026flt}. Writing the proof is no longer the
bottleneck.

What remains is the statement. A formal proof proves what its statement
says, and checking that the statement, and the definitions it depends
on, say what was meant needs a person. Generated formalizations are not
written to be read, so this check is still slow. As
\citet{hales2026reliability} puts it, a proof in Lean should not be
accepted until a human has checked statement fidelity, a task much
easier than checking the proof. For Fermat's Last Theorem the check was
easy, because the generated statement could be compared mechanically
with one that people had already written and checked in Mathlib. Most
results have no such statement to compare with. We would like the
check to stay easy anyway.

In this paper we take one step towards this, in one area of quantum
computing where formalization is wanted: completeness of equational
theories for circuits. A quantum circuit denotes a unitary matrix. A
set of equations between circuits is \emph{sound} if both sides of
each equation denote the same matrix, and \emph{complete} if every
equation between circuits with the same matrix follows from it. For example, Selinger's fifteen relations
for the gates $\omega$, $H$, $S$ and $CZ$ are complete for $n$-qubit
Clifford circuits, for every $n$~\cite{selinger2015clifford}. Soundness
is a finite check. Completeness says that the equations present the
group or monoid of matrices that the circuits generate, and it is much
harder to prove.

There are a few standard ways to prove it. One defines a normal form,
shows that every circuit can be rewritten to one, and shows that
different normal forms have different matrices. Another starts from an
exact synthesis algorithm, which turns a matrix into a word, and shows
by induction on a measure of the matrix that every generator applied to
it can be absorbed into the algorithm's output. A third moves a known
complete theory along a translation between circuit languages. In each
case the core of the proof is a case analysis: every relation against
every normal form, or every generator against every shape of matrix
column. The papers say so in their page counts. The extended version of
Hadamard-$\Pi$~\cite{fang2026hadamard} has 116 pages, most of them on
the completeness of one set of relations. Makary, Ross and Selinger
support their completeness proof for real stabilizer circuits with a
supplement of 76 pages of derivations~\cite{makary2021real}. Cl\'ement's
completeness theorem for real Clifford+$CH$ circuits has a 13-page text
and an appendix of 264 pages~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch}. Most
of these papers appear at conferences, where a referee has a few weeks
for several papers, so in practice such appendices are seldom checked
in full. An author cannot easily check them again after a change
either.

These papers have one thing in common that helps: they share a
semantics. A circuit is a word over gates, it denotes a matrix, and the
theorem says that the relations present the group, or monoid, that the
matrices of the circuits form. So the form of the statement is the same
in every paper. We use an Agda library, described in an earlier
paper~\cite{anonymous2027popl}, that fixes this form once: words,
circuits on $n$ wires with their structural rules, and what it means for
relations to present a group or a subgroup of one. On top of it an LLM
coding agent wrote the formalizations, under our direction, except the
qudit Clifford development, which was written mainly by hand. For each
paper, a person then checks only what the library cannot fix: the
relations as transcribed, the matrices of the gates, the interpretation
of a circuit as a matrix, and a theorem statement of one line. For the
real stabilizer development these come to under 500 of its 11{,}799
lines. Agda checks the rest.

We did this for thirteen completeness proofs, written between 2014 and
2026, and for the minimality theorem of a fourteenth paper.
Table~\ref{tab:corpus} lists them. Each development states its result
against a semantics: the relations present the group of matrices that
the generators generate, or they present a submonoid of a matrix group,
at every width or dimension. The one exception is the qudit Clifford
group of \citet{bian2026qupit}, whose relations are shown to present an
algebraic model of that group (Section~\ref{sec:qupit}). A development
has about 22{,}000 lines on average, and all of them together about
1{,}100 modules and 300{,}000 lines, on a library of 16{,}000 lines
that we extended where we needed to. They typecheck in safe Agda~2.8
with no postulates.

The most important finding is about Hadamard-$\Pi$, a POPL~2026 paper
on equational reasoning for quantum programs~\cite{fang2026hadamard}.
Its Theorem~4.14 says that the relations of its Figure~6 present the
group $O_n(\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}])$ of orthogonal $n\times n$ matrices with
entries in $\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$. The proof is incomplete. Two of its lemmas
are false, one of the cases of its Main Lemma relies on them, and that
case fails on an explicit state in dimension~9 (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
The theorem is still true. An unpublished thesis of Cl\'ement from
2019~\cite{clement2019thesis} presents the same group by a different
set of relations, and handles the problematic case by other diagrams.
We proved that the two sets of relations generate the same congruence,
formalized Cl\'ement's argument for the missing case, and later
formalized his whole proof. Cl\'ement's 2026 paper on real
Clifford+$CH$ circuits imports exactly Theorem~4.14 of Hadamard-$\Pi$ as
its Theorem~4.4, so its main theorem also rested on this gap. Our
formalization of that paper discharges the import, so its completeness
theorem is now proved with no hypothesis.

The other papers are correct, but they contain misprints, and some of
them are in statements that proofs use. Eleven of the 137 typed
relations in the appendix of the real stabilizer paper are false as
printed~\cite{makary2021real}. One relation in Table~2 of Li, Ross and
Selinger's presentation of $O_n(\Z[\frac12])$ has its exponents
exchanged~\cite{li2021on}. A scalar in Figure~4 of Blake's simpler
presentations~\cite{blake2026simpler} makes one rule unsound. Our own
transcriptions were wrong too, sometimes in ways that only a later step
of the formalization exposed. Section~\ref{sec:findings} lists all of
these.

The qudit Clifford development was written mainly by a person, over three
months. Everything else took 24 days, and there the agent wrote the
Agda and the scripts that generated the long derivations. What made this possible is that almost every claim in
these papers can be checked by a computation before it is formalized,
and that Agda checks the result after. We describe how we used both in
Section~\ref{sec:method}.

With language models, we need new ways of engaging with mathematics and
its formalization. We think that scaffolds made by people, on which the
models build, are one step in this direction: the people check the
scaffold once and a short statement per result, and the machine checks
the rest.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A way of formalizing a family of papers with an LLM agent: a
  library fixes the form of the theorem, and a person checks a short
  statement per paper (Sections~\ref{sec:corpus} and~\ref{sec:agent}).
\item Machine-checked proofs of thirteen completeness theorems for
  quantum circuits and two-level matrix groups, and of one minimality
  theorem, each stated against a matrix semantics or, for qudit
  Clifford circuits, an algebraic model (Section~\ref{sec:corpus}).
\item A gap in the completeness proof of Hadamard-$\Pi$, with explicit
  counterexamples to two of its lemmas and to one case of its Main
  Lemma, and two machine-checked repairs (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
\item A list of the other errors that the formalization found, in the
  papers and in our own transcriptions (Section~\ref{sec:findings}).
\item The proof architectures behind these results, as they appear in
  Agda: completeness by levels and edges for exact synthesis,
  inductive normal forms for circuits, induction on the width,
  products and extensions of presentations, and encodings between
  circuit languages (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).
\item The methods we used to produce the proofs, and what they cost in
  Agda, including the performance problems we met and how we solved
  them (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:engineering}).
\end{itemize}
