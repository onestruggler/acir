\section{Proof Routes, and What Each Asks of the Author}\label{sec:architecture}

The fourteen sources use few proof methods. We call a method as it
appears in Agda a route; two of the routes are module shapes, copied
from development to development, and the others are patterns. This
section describes them, and for each says what an author who brings a
new theory has to supply and what the machine supplies. The route is
the paper's choice, not ours: a paper that proves completeness by a
normal form is formalized by a normal form. For a new theory the route
follows what the author has. An exact synthesis algorithm over the
ring gives the levels route; a candidate normal form the normal-form
route; a decomposition of circuits by wires the induction on the
width; a complete theory for a nearby language an encoding. Four of
the shapes below are such routes. The other two, products of
presentations and Reidemeister--Schreier ports, compose presentations
rather than prove one from scratch. Section~\ref{sec:example} follows
one development from its gate set to its theorem, and
Section~\ref{sec:generated} tabulates, across the developments, how
much was generated and how much was written.

\subsection{Levels and edges}\label{sec:levels}

The two-level developments share the following definitions (the two
earliest state the induction slightly differently, but with the same
\aid{Path}). Here \aid{nw M o} is the algorithm's word for
a matrix \aid{M} with orthonormal columns \aid{o}, \aid{actMʷ} is the
action of a word on a matrix, and \aid{level} the level of
Section~\ref{sec:by-levels}.
\begin{agdacode}
Path : (w : Word (Gen n)) (M : Matrix n n D) → .(ColOrth M) → Set
Path w M o = nw (actMʷ w M) (ColOrth-actMʷ w o) • w ≈ nw M o

EdgeStep : Set
EdgeStep = ∀ L → EdgesBelow L → EdgesAt L
\end{agdacode}
Here \aid{EdgesBelow L} says that every generator is a path from every
state when both ends of the edge are below $L$, and \aid{EdgesAt L}
the same for the edges out of a state at $L$ that do not go up. A proof
of \aid{EdgeStep} is the Main Lemma. From it, a generic
module proves completeness: by well-founded induction on the
lexicographic order of levels, every edge is a path, so every word is a
path, and two words with the same matrix are related. The orthogonality
proof \aid{o} is irrelevant, so that the normal word depends on the
matrix alone.

Three details of this formulation matter in practice. First, the
statement covers every generator, not only the basic ones that the
papers' Main Lemmas are stated for. The papers reduce the other
generators to the basic ones in a sentence; in Agda this reduction is a
module of its own, because conjugating by an exchange must be shown to
keep the state at or below its level. Second, squares are closed by one
lemma, \aid{close}, which takes the two syllables $N$ and $N'$, the
word $W$, the proof that every state on the path of $W$ is below $L$,
and the relation $W N' g \approx N$. Many cases of a Main Lemma are
one call to \aid{close} with a relation and a list of level facts.
Third, the definitions are generic in the normal word. When we found
the gap in Hadamard-$\Pi$, the proof of the missing case was written
over any function \aid{nw} with three properties (paths compose, edges
below $L$ are paths, and the non-hard edges at $L$ are paths), so that
the same proof served both Hadamard-$\Pi$'s algorithm and Cl\'ement's.

The level facts are where Agda's evaluator helps and also where it
hurts. The routes in these proofs touch at most eight entries of the
pivot column, and leave the rest alone. We describe such a state as a fixed
numerator with a window of entries replaced, a \emph{known state}, and
derive its level from the parities of the window alone. Each step of a
route is then one ring identity, $a\pm b = \sqrt2\, c$, about symbolic
entries, which a script generates and a ring solver proves. The
evaluator must never see the full column with its symbolic entries,
because it would try to compute the level, and the ring operations of
$\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$, records of dyadic fractions, are expensive to
unfold (Section~\ref{sec:engineering}).

What the author supplies on this route is the choice of ring, the
action of the generators on a column, the algorithm \aid{nw} with the
proof that $\mathit{nw}(M)\cdot M=I$, the level with the proof that a
syllable lowers it, and \aid{EdgeStep}, which is the Main Lemma. The
well-founded induction, the composition of paths, \aid{close} and the
presentation record are the shape, written once and copied. Inside the
Main Lemma the machine does the arithmetic: a script generates the
ring identities of each route and the ring solver proves them, and
where a case analysis is large it is data, a decision tree checked by
evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:trees}). The relations that a square
needs are derived by the rewriter of Section~\ref{sec:search}, by
search where the paper gives no derivation.

\subsection{Normal forms for circuits}\label{sec:nf}

The Clifford and real stabilizer developments follow their papers'
normal forms closely, and share a structure. The normal forms are
inductive types: ladders, $Z$-circuits, $D$-ladders and $X$-circuits,
indexed in the real case by a \emph{wire type} that records whether
$Z$ alone or both $X$ and $Z$ are tracked on a wire. A normal form on
$n+1$ wires is a $Z$-circuit and an $X$-circuit that reach down to the
bottom wire, followed by a normal form on $n$ wires shifted up.
Rewriting a word to normal form pushes one gate at a time through the
parts of a normal form, by a mutual recursion whose tables are the
papers' typed relations; in the Clifford development the tables are
generated from those relations and checked letter by letter, and in
the real case they were written by hand, each entry paired with its
relation so that a wrong entry does not typecheck. Uniqueness is
semantic, by the conjugation action on Pauli operators. In the real
case, a normal form's action on $Z_0$ determines its $Z$-circuit, its
action on $X_0$ the $X$-circuit, and the rest acts one wire up. The
matrix semantics is the layer's circuit end over a commutative ring
with an involution and an element $s$ with $2s^2=1$ that the involution
fixes (Section~\ref{sec:matrix-layer}). One instantiation is
$\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$, which connects these developments to the
two-level ones (Section~\ref{sec:theorem-form}). Another is the field
$\mathbb{F}_7$, with $s=2$, which shows that the hypotheses of the
generation theorem are consistent; the intended model is $\mathbb{R}$,
which Agda does not have.

The author supplies the normal forms as inductive types, the push of
one gate through each part of a normal form, and the decoding of a
normal form from its action, which is where the paper's uniqueness
argument goes. This route has no module shape: the author states these
obligations for their own normal form, and the library's lemma that
soundness and a unique normal form give completeness closes the
argument, or a page of direct proof does, as in
Section~\ref{sec:example}. The typed relations that the push uses are
generated from the supplement, and soundness is decided by evaluation.

\subsection{Induction on the width}\label{sec:width}

The CNOT-dihedral and phase-affine developments prove completeness by
induction on the number of wires, with a decomposition that the papers
use in different forms. Every circuit is related to
$D\cdot X_b \cdot L$, a diagonal part, a column of $X$ gates, and a
linear circuit. Two circuits with the same matrix have the same $b$;
their linear parts are equal by a coset tower in the style of
Lafont~\cite{lafont2003boolean}, which needs completeness one wire
down; and their diagonal parts are equal because a diagonal part
splits into a part one wire up and a form on the bottom wire, and that
form is determined by its phase function.

The phase-affine hierarchy~\cite{blake2026phaseaffine} has four levels.
We organized level~3, which adds $T$, around a functor. It goes from circuits of a lower
level to circuits of level~3 one wire up, with the new wire~0 as a
control. It maps every derivation to a derivation, and its semantics
multiplies the phase by a polynomial in the control bit. With it, the
bottom-wire forms of level~3 are $T^t$ followed by the images of a
level-1 and a level-2 circuit, and their uniqueness follows from
completeness at levels~1 and~2, which are proved first.

The author supplies the decomposition, the coset tower of the linear
part, and the uniqueness of the bottom-wire form. In the CNOT-dihedral
development the local identities that the tower and the decomposition
use, on two and three wires, were found by breadth-first search and
written out as derivations, soundness is decided on tables, and a
reflective normalizer decides the equations between diagonal
expressions that the decomposition meets. In the phase-affine
development the dimension is symbolic, so soundness is by evaluation on
symbolic labels, and the ring identities of its monomial calculus are
generated by a script.

\subsection{Products, extensions and isomorphisms}\label{sec:products}

Most of the qudit Clifford development is assembled from constructions of the
library~\cite{anonymous2027popl}. The long case analysis is confined to
the symplectic normal form, and three constructions do the rest.
The first is the semidirect product: given presentations of $N$ and
$H$ and, for each generator $h$ of $H$ and $n$ of $N$, a word $c(h,n)$
over $N$, the library presents $N\rtimes H$ by the relations of both
and $h\,n = c(h,n)\,h$, once the client has shown the action well
defined on the relations of $H$ and of $N$; the normal forms of the
product are pairs of normal forms, by the Reidemeister--Schreier
method with the words over $H$ as cosets. The second is the extension,
which need not split: a presentation of a group $G$ with a normal
subgroup $N$ adds to the relations of $N$ the conjugates of its
generators by the representatives, and each relation of $G/N$ twisted
by the word in $N$ that its lift picks up. For the qudit Clifford group
these corrections come from the Weyl cocycle; the correction of a
relation is a phase in $\Z_p$, computed by folding the product law of
the Heisenberg--Weyl group over the two sides of the relation, and
only $(SH)^3$ has a nonzero one, $(p^2-1)/8$, which is $-\frac18$
modulo $p$ because $p^2\equiv1 \pmod 8$. The third is transport along
isomorphisms of rule sets: when translations of generators to words
carry the relations of each rule set to equations derivable in the
other, and the two composites are the identity up to derivation, a
presentation theorem moves from one rule set to the other. This is
how Figure~1 inherits its theorem from the rule set where the
mathematics was done, and why the second form of C4 cost little.

Here the author supplies presentations of the factors, and the
well-definedness of the action or the cocycle on the relations; the
product's presentation and its normal form come from the library. A
variant of a rule set costs the translation of its generators and the
derivations of the rules that differ.

\subsection{Encodings, and the bridge to Theorem 4.4}\label{sec:encoding}

Cl\'ement's real Clifford+$CH$ paper~\cite{clement2026realcliffordch}
proves completeness at each width $n\ge2$ by a back-and-forth argument.
There is a second language $Y$ with a complete theory, an encoding $e$
from circuits to $Y$ that preserves the matrix, and a decoding $d$ back
such that $d(e(g)) \approx g$ for every gate (its Lemma~8.7) and $d$
maps every relation of $Y$ to a derivable equation (its Lemma~8.8).
Then two circuits with the same matrix have encodings with the same
matrix, hence related encodings, hence related decodings, hence are
related. We proved this argument once, generically, and instantiated
it at $n=2$, where $Y$ is the language of two-level matrices on four
basis vectors, and at $n\ge3$, where $Y$ is the paper's group $P$. In
the second case, completeness of $Y$ is the paper's Theorem~4.10,
which it proves from Theorem~4.4 by the Reidemeister--Schreier method
with four cosets. We formalized that too. Its two families of
obligations are exactly the paper's items (a) and (b), and most of the
work went into item (b), where we found that the coset action is a
conjugation of a uniform embedding by four fixed words, so that each
obligation reduces to the image of one relation.

That left Theorem~4.4: the relations of Figure~6 are complete for
\emph{proper} words, those whose letters have distinct indices, at two
semantics, on 4 basis vectors and on $2^n$. The paper cites
Hadamard-$\Pi$ for it, and our Hadamard-$\Pi$ development proves the
same statement, but the two developments were written independently
and differ in every detail: generators with indices in either order
against generators with increasing indices; matrices as functions of
bit vectors over a hand-made $\Z[\sqrt2]$, read with a power of
$\frac1{\sqrt2}$, against EucDomain's $\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$ indexed by
$\mathsf{Fin}\,n$; basis vectors numbered by a Gray code at the width
of $P$, and by a table on two qubits. The bridge has a ring map
$\iota$ from $\Z[\sqrt2]$ to $\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$; a translation
$\varphi$ from proper letters to increasing words, which writes a
decreasing letter by the paper's rules (e1) and (e2), with a map $\psi$
back that sends each relation of Hadamard-$\Pi$'s Figure~6 to the same
relation of the paper's, so that $\psi(\varphi(u))\approx u$; and, for
any numbering of the basis by $\mathsf{Fin}\,2^n$, a map $\Phi$ from
scaled matrices $(\ell,M)$ to matrices over $\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$, with
entry $(\frac1{\sqrt2})^\ell\,\iota(M(r)(c))$ at the numbered
positions. $\Phi$ is a monoid homomorphism, its sums over bit vectors
being sums over $\mathsf{Fin}\,2^n$ renumbered by a permutation, for
which the standard library's \aid{sum-permute} applies; it identifies
the pairs that the paper's localization identifies; and it sends the
matrix of each generator to the matrix of its translation, which we
checked entry by entry, on two qubits by a generated module of 336
cases. Then two proper words with the same matrix have translations
with the same matrix, which are related by the Hadamard-$\Pi$
development, so the words are related after $\psi$. With both
instances of Theorem~4.4 proved, the paper's Theorem~8.9 holds with no
hypothesis:
\begin{agdacode}
completeness : ∀ n → Complete n
subpresentationᴼ : ∀ n → (n VRel,_===_) IsSubPresentationOf O (2 ^ n)
\end{agdacode}
Here \aid{O (2 \textasciicircum{} n)} is the orthogonal group
$O_{2^n}(\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}])$ of the Hadamard-$\Pi$ development. The
development first proved the theorem for a monoid: it reads a circuit
as a pair of an integer matrix over $\Z[\sqrt2]$ and a power of
$\frac1{\sqrt2}$, which is fast to compute with, and such pairs have no
inverses. For the group version the bridge's $\Phi$ carries each pair
to a matrix over $\Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}]$, which is orthogonal because
every gate matrix is symmetric and every gate is its own inverse by
Cl\'ement's rules, and $\Phi$ is injective up to the equality of pairs
because $\iota$ is injective and $\sqrt2$ is a unit. So the real
Clifford+$CH$ circuits, the real stabilizer circuits and Figure~6 of
Hadamard-$\Pi$ are now all stated against the same group.

The author supplies the encoding, the decoding, the proof that the
encoding preserves the matrix, the two lemmas of the back-and-forth
argument, and the completeness of the target theory; the argument
itself is one generic module of a hundred lines. Decision procedures on
three qubits pin the transcription of the encoding and of the target
theory's rules before any of this is proved
(Section~\ref{sec:numerical}), and the auxiliary equations of the
decoding, several hundred in Cl\'ement's paper, are proved by steps
that are each justified by completeness one width down, decided by
evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:eval}), or a piece of word algebra.

\subsection{Ports of existing Agda proofs}\label{sec:ports}

Bian and Selinger's Agda proofs for two-qubit Clifford+$T$ and
three-qubit Clifford+$CS$ circuits~\cite{bian2023cliffordt2,bian2023cliffordcs3}
were written against their own tactic library. We rebased that library
on our words and presentations, so that their generated proof terms
port mechanically, and then connected their syntactic theorem to the
two-level presentations of Greylyn and of Bian and Selinger. For the
three-qubit result, the original proof checks five modules with Agda's
call-by-name evaluation. After a change to the tactic library
(Section~\ref{sec:engineering}), four of them check with the default
strategy, and the fifth followed once the coset normal forms were
computed incrementally; the whole development now checks in about five
minutes.

What a Reidemeister--Schreier argument asks of the author is the
translation of generators, the coset action, and the two families of
obligations, items (a) and (b) as in Section~\ref{sec:encoding}; the
theorem that these give injectivity is in the library, in the original
authors' own interface, and the obligations are what their generated
proof terms discharge.

\subsection{A worked example: real stabilizer circuits}\label{sec:example}

The real stabilizer development~\cite{makary2021real} is the one whose
statement Section~\ref{sec:theorem-form} reads. Here we follow it from
the gate set to the theorem, and say at each step who did the work.
The steps, in order: write the gates and the rules; choose the ring and
the gate matrices, and check the rules against the paper numerically;
prove soundness by evaluation; choose the route and state its
obligations; generate the derivations and tables, or search for them
where the paper gives none; prove the route's mathematics; fill the
record. Its route is the paper's, normal forms.

\paragraph{The gate set and the relations.} The gates are $-1$, $H$, $Z$
and $CZ$, a gate set is a family indexed by arity, and the relations
R1--R16 of the paper's Figure~1 are the constructors of an inductive
family, each stated at the width it is drawn on:
\begin{agdacode}
data Gate : ℕ → Set where
  neg-gate       : Gate 0
  H-gate Z-gate  : Gate 1
  CZ-gate        : Gate 2

data _SRel,_===_ : (n : ℕ) → WRel (Gen n) where
  R₁  : n SRel, neg • neg === ε
  R₄  : (₁₊ n) SRel, (H • Z) ^ 4 === neg
  R₁₃ : (₃₊ n) SRel, CZ • CZ ↑ === CZ ↑ • CZ
  ⋯
\end{agdacode}
Wire 0 is the bottom wire and words are in operator order, which the
module says in its header. \aid{Lift-Relation} adds the structural
rules, and one line per generator names its inverse, so that the
presented monoid is a group. This module is 117 lines, and it is the
first thing a reader compares with the paper.

\paragraph{The matrices.} The semantics is the layer's circuit end over
any commutative ring with an involution and an element $s$ with
$2s^2=1$. The development gives the four gate matrices, $H$ as $s$
times an integer matrix, and reads a generator on a higher wire by the
layer's shift:
\begin{agdacode}
valA : Gen n → Op n
valA (gate₀ neg-gate) = scal (- 1#)
valA (gate₁ H-gate)   = emb (scale s (ιO (ZI.ix hZ)))
valA (gate₁ Z-gate)   = emb (ιO (ZI.ix zZ))
valA (gate₂ CZ-gate)  = emb (ιO (ZI.ix czZ))
valA (g ↥)            = up (valA g)
\end{agdacode}
with \aid{hZ}, \aid{zZ} and \aid{czZ} the integer matrices and
\aid{ιO} their image in the ring. A circuit is the product of its
letters. These 30 lines are the second thing a reader compares with
the paper, and the first thing we checked against it numerically
(Section~\ref{sec:numerical}).

\paragraph{Soundness.} Each relation is checked at the width it is
drawn on by evaluation: the integer matrix of the left side is a power
of 2 times that of the right, and the powers of $s$ account for the
difference. Each of the sixteen checks is \aid{refl}; what was written
is the clause that names the relation's two sides and its power of 2,
127 lines in all, and the structural rules follow from the layer's
lemmas.

\paragraph{The normal form, and the route's obligations.} The paper's
Definitions 4.1--4.10 become inductive types of ladders, $Z$-circuits,
$D$-ladders and $X$-circuits (139 lines). The route then asks for three
things, which the author states for their own normal form, the library
having no module for this route (Section~\ref{sec:nf}):
\begin{agdacode}
normalise-ok : (n : ℕ) (w : Circuit n) → n ⊢ w ≈ ⟦ normalise n w ⟧ⁿ
act-unique   : (N N′ : NF n) → ⟦ N ⟧ⁿ ≗ᵃ ⟦ N′ ⟧ⁿ → unsign N ≡ unsign N′
act-of-mat   : {w v : Circuit n} → ⟦ w ⟧ᴬ ≐ ⟦ v ⟧ᴬ → w ≗ᵃ v
\end{agdacode}
The first is the paper's Proposition~5.7: every circuit equals its
normal form. It is a mutual recursion that pushes one generator at a
time through the three parts of a normal form, 821 lines, and each of
its steps is one typed relation. The second is its Propositions
4.11--4.13: normal forms that act alike on Pauli operators by
conjugation are equal up to their sign, proved by decoding the
$Z$-circuit from the image of $Z_0$ and the $X$-circuit from the image
of $X_0$, 473 lines with the action on Pauli operators. The third
connects the matrix to the action, 467 lines: the matrix of a circuit
conjugates Pauli matrices as the action says, and a Pauli matrix is
determined by its entries. From the three, \aid{completeness} is a
page, and the sign is settled by $-U\neq U$ in a nontrivial ring. These
1{,}900 lines are the core of the paper's mathematics; with the tables,
the axioms as letter lists and the reasoning at a fixed width it comes
to 2{,}541 lines (Table~\ref{tab:example}), and that is what the
author writes.

\paragraph{The typed relations.} The push uses 137 typed relations, one
per gate and kind of normal-form wire, which the paper lists in its
Appendix~B and derives in its supplement, together with 19 lemmas. A
script, written for this development and not kept, followed the
supplement's derivations line by line for the 19 lemmas and 90 of the
typed relations and emitted each as a list of rewriter steps
(Section~\ref{sec:search}); eight further typed relations were derived
by normalizing both sides, and two by hand. With the 137 rules that
restate them in the form the push uses, these derivations are 264 in
all and 5{,}995 lines, half of the development, checked by the
rewriter, 316 lines of which each development with derivations has its
own copy; eleven of them end in a corrected relation
(Appendix~\ref{sec:misprints}). The tables that index them by the
types of the gates (456 lines) were written by hand, with each entry
paired with its relation, so that a wrong entry does not typecheck.

\paragraph{The theorem.} The last module (130 lines) shows that the
matrix of a circuit is unitary, by evaluation on the gate matrices and
the inverse of a word, and fills the record:
\begin{agdacode}
presentation : {n : ℕ} → (n VRel,_===_) IsSubPresentationOf U (2 ^ n)
presentation {n} = record
  { gl   = grouplike
  ; ⟦_⟧  = ⟦_⟧ᵘ
  ; mono = record { isGroupHomomorphism = ⋯
                  ; injective = λ e → completeness (mat-injective e) } }
\end{agdacode}
The homomorphism is the layer's: a monoid homomorphism between groups
is a group homomorphism, and the matrix of a product is the product of
the matrices. Injectivity is completeness after the layer's
\aid{mat-injective}, which says that the matrix determines the
operator. That line is the last of the four things of
Section~\ref{sec:theorem-form} that the reader checks; the third, the
group, is the layer's and is checked once. The guarantee is the
statement of Section~\ref{sec:theorem-form}: R1--R16 present the
subgroup of $U_{2^n}$ that the four gate matrices generate, over every
ring with the stated properties. That this subgroup is the paper's
real Clifford group, the orthogonal matrices that normalize the Pauli
group, is the generation theorem, proved separately; and two further
modules instantiate the statement at $\D[\omega]$ and at $\D[\sqrt2]$.

\paragraph{The cost.} Table~\ref{tab:example} sorts the 11{,}799 lines
by who wrote them. Nearly all of the development's commits fall within
four hours. The theorem and its $\D[\omega]$ instance typecheck from
scratch in about 75 seconds, and the $\D[\sqrt2]$ instance takes about
100 seconds more, given the Hadamard-$\Pi$ and Cl\'ement developments.
Half of its lines are generated, a fifth are the paper's mathematics,
a seventh are results beyond the theorem (the count of Corollary~4.16,
and the generation theorem of Section~\ref{sec:nf}), and the layer,
the rewriter, the statements and the soundness checks are the rest.

\subsection{What is generated and what is written}\label{sec:generated}

Table~\ref{tab:generated} gives, for each development, how many lines
were generated by a script and what they are. What is generated is
what the paper itself presents as a computation: a derivation, a
diagram with its levels, a case analysis over residues, and in the
Clifford development the rewriting tables. What is written is what the
paper presents as an idea: a normal form, a decoding, a level, a
decomposition, a conjugation that makes two cases one. The share of
generated code is high where the paper's derivations were followed
line by line, and low where its arguments were replaced by structure,
as in the real Clifford+$CH$ development, whose bulk is word algebra
written by the agent with completeness one width down as a black box,
and where several thousand further lines are decided by evaluation
rather than generated. Nothing generated is trusted: the script can be
wrong, and Agda checks its output as it checks everything else.
