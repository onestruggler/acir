\section{How the Proofs Were Produced}\label{sec:method}

Thirteen of the developments were written within 24 days, from 19
September to 12 October 2026, in 307 commits, with several often in
progress at once. Judging by their commits, most of them took less
than a day of work each, often a few hours: the
Clifford and real stabilizer developments have nearly all their
commits within two and four hours, and Cl\'ement's thesis within one
day. Hadamard-$\Pi$ took three days, and the real Clifford+$CH$
development, by far the largest, was worked on through most of the
period. The qudit Clifford
development was written earlier, from July to early October 2026, in
457 commits, alongside the paper it accompanies. It was written mainly
by hand; an LLM coding agent helped with parts of it and is a co-author
of about a fifth of those commits. The pace of the other thirteen
depended on four habits, which we describe in turn, and on an LLM
coding agent, which we describe after them.

\subsection{Check numerically first}\label{sec:numerical}

Almost every statement in these papers is a claim about finitely many
explicit matrices: a relation between two circuits, a typed relation
between a gate and a normal-form layer, a diagram whose states and
levels can be computed, an intermediate row of a derivation. Before
formalizing a statement we checked it by computation in Python, exact
over $\Z[\sqrt2]$ where levels or residues were involved, and compared
it with the printed page. This is fast, and when it fails the problem
is in the statement, not in the proof we are about to write. Most of
the misprints of Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-errata} were found this
way; the others were found by the decision procedures of
Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

The same scripts look for counterexamples. The counterexamples to
Lemmas~A.16 and~A.17 of Hadamard-$\Pi$, and the state on which its Main
Case~9 fails, were found by a script that samples random states of a
given level and follows the case analysis of the proof, computing the
level of every state on every diagram. For Cl\'ement's real
Clifford+$CH$ appendix we wrote a small floating-point simulator of
circuits on a few qubits, and tested every intermediate claim with it
before writing the corresponding Agda. A similar script, computing the
signed permutation of a swap network, found our own transcription error
in Section~\ref{sec:own-errors}.

\subsection{Derivations by search}\label{sec:search}

The supplements of the Clifford and real stabilizer papers, the
diagrams of Cl\'ement's thesis, and the appendix of Li, Ross and
Selinger contain long derivations: chains of words, each obtained from
the previous by one relation, up to commuting letters. We did not
transcribe these by hand. A reflective rewriter in Agda represents a
word as a list of letters and checks a derivation given as a list of
steps:
\begin{agdacode}
δxpp = derive (x13 ∷ h02 ∷ h13 ∷ h01 ∷ h23 ∷ []) (h02 ∷ h13 ∷ h01 ∷ h23 ∷ x23 ∷ [])
  ( perm (x13 ∷ h02 ∷ h13 ∷ h01 ∷ h23 ∷ [])
  ∷ rw 0 (sym⁼ (ʟa3 f1 f3 lt13))
  ∷ perm (h13 ∷ h13 ∷ x13 ∷ h02 ∷ h13 ∷ h01 ∷ h23 ∷ [])
  ∷ rw 1 (sym⁼ (ʟd2 f1 f3 lt13))
  ∷ ⋯ ) refl
\end{agdacode}
A \aid{perm} step moves to a word equal up to commuting letters, which
the rewriter decides by computing canonical forms. A \aid{rw i e} step
rewrites at position \aid{i} with an equation \aid{e}. Equations can be
reversed, shifted up a wire, read backwards (every letter is an
involution in most of these theories), rotated as relators, and moved
to any increasing choice of indices. The final \aid{refl} asks Agda to
run the checker on the closed list of steps.

A Python script produces the steps. It follows the paper's derivation
line by line: for each printed line it searches for a \aid{perm} and a
\aid{rw} that reach it from the previous one, using the relation the
paper cites first and the others if that fails. When no step reaches a
printed line, the line or its citation is wrong, which is how the
citation errors of Appendix~\ref{sec:slips} showed up. Where a paper
gives no derivation, the script searches for one by breadth-first
search over rewrites with the local relations, with intermediate
lemmas to keep the search small. The scripts for the real
Clifford+$CH$ development and for Cl\'ement's thesis are in the
repository and regenerate their modules byte for byte; the scripts of
the other five developments with generated code were not kept. An
author of a new theory writes such a script, or has the agent write
one, following a derivation where the paper gives one and searching
where it does not. The generated modules are a large part of some
developments (Table~\ref{tab:generated}): the real stabilizer
development derives the 137 typed relations this way, the Clifford
development the 101 equations of its supplement, and the proof of
Lemma~A.1 in the real Clifford+$CH$ development has chains of 101 and
170 steps. We split such chains into segments between explicit lines;
a single chain of 101 steps exhausted 4\,GB, because each step's type
normalizes all the steps before it.

\subsection{Case analyses as decision trees}\label{sec:trees}

Cl\'ement's Case~3.4 is a case analysis over symbolic entries. The four
odd entries of the pivot column are written as linear forms over
$\Z[\sqrt2]$ in a few integer variables. The cases split on the parity
of a form, on the residue class of a further entry outside the window,
or on a symmetry, and every leaf is one of his diagrams with signs
added. We wrote the analysis as data: a decision tree with nodes for
splits, for new entries at a given depth and class, for relabellings
of the window, for conjugation by a letter that commutes with the hard
edge, and for a change of variables into a normal form. A Boolean
function checks a tree against the forms, and a soundness theorem says
that a tree that passes the check gives the path. The trees themselves
are checked by \aid{refl}.

The trees are generated, and their size depended on how the search was
organized. A greedy search over the original variables gave a tree with
more than 5{,}000 leaves. Bringing every case first into Cl\'ement's
normal form (38), by signs, relabellings and a change of variables,
gave one shared subtree of 202 leaves for his Subcase~3.4.2 and a small
top-level tree that reaches it. Agda checks the shared subtree in
about 17~seconds.

\subsection{Proof by evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

Many obligations in these developments are equations between explicit
matrices: soundness of a relation, a step justified by completeness one
width down, an identity of $8\times8$ integer matrices. The obvious
proof is \aid{refl} on two stored tries. For the real Clifford+$CH$
development this was too slow. The conversion checker compares the
tries entry by entry, and does not share work between entries, so it
evaluates the product of dense matrices once for each entry. We
replaced it by a Boolean decision procedure on a reading of the
circuit as row operations, with a soundness lemma: the proof is
\aid{refl : eqM A B ≡ true}. This made the soundness of the relations
go from 525.6 to 1.2 seconds, Lemma~D.5 of the appendix from about an
hour to 54 seconds, and one 80-gate equation on three wires from 158
seconds to under one. The same shape works for the decision procedures
that check Figure~8 of the same paper on three qubits at every
admissible index. There, each module holds a few thousand evaluations,
because Agda frees the memory of an evaluation only when its module is
done.

\subsection{An LLM coding agent}\label{sec:agent}

The Agda in the developments other than the qudit Clifford development,
and the Python
scripts, were written by Claude Code, an LLM coding agent, in sessions
that we directed. We chose
the papers, decided what to formalize and in what order, and stated
the targets; the agent read the papers, wrote and ran the scripts and
the Agda, and kept notes in the repository. Agda's checker is the only
part of this that we trust. Every theorem typechecks under
\texttt{-{}-safe}, with no postulates and no holes, so a proof that is
accepted is correct relative to its statement, whoever wrote it.

The statements are a different matter. A wrong definition is checked
as faithfully as a right one, and the false hypothesis of
Section~\ref{sec:own-errors} stayed in the development, unnoticed, until
we tried to discharge it. For this reason every result ends in a short
module that states it without hypotheses, in the form of
Section~\ref{sec:theorem-form}: the relation set over the library's
words, the group from the layer, and the interpretation in the
record's field. These are the statements a reader should check, and
the scaffold is what keeps them short. What the scaffold does not fix,
the relations and the gate matrices, the agent wrote from the paper;
we read the relations against the paper and checked the matrices by
computation. A library of matrices for the common gates would remove
the second step, and is the next addition to make.

We also found that the agent's mistakes resembled human ones.
It misread a diagram at low resolution, took the bounds of a product in
the wrong order, and stated a hypothesis more strongly than the paper
does. Each was caught by a computation or by Agda, not by the agent's
own reading. The numerical checks of Section~\ref{sec:numerical} were
also the agent's main check on its own work before Agda saw it.

\subsection{Beyond checking}\label{sec:beyond}

A colleague asked whether these tools help with the mathematics, and
not only with checking it. In a few ways they did.

\paragraph{Missing conditions.} Three of our findings are conditions
assumed without being stated, one by a paper and two by us: the
distinctness conventions of Cl\'ement's Figure~8; the proper words of
its Theorem~4.4, which the paper does state and our first statement
dropped; and the hypothesis that scalars commute with one another,
which an earlier version of our own circuit layer assumed and which
the independence models showed cannot be dropped from the structural
rules (Section~\ref{sec:independence-layer}). The first and third were
found when a decision procedure, or a model, was run on the statement
as written; the second when we tried to discharge the hypothesis.

\paragraph{Rule sets.} A presentation theorem transports along an
isomorphism of rule sets (Section~\ref{sec:products}), so a variant
rule set costs the translation of its generators and the derivation of
the rules that differ: the four rule sets of the qudit Clifford
development and both spellings of its rule C4 were related this way,
and so were Cl\'ement's thesis relations and Figure~6 of
Hadamard-$\Pi$, in every dimension. That a rule is not redundant is
shown by a model and one lemma, which is how Blake's six minimality
theorems and our own structural rules were done.

\paragraph{Shorter proofs.} Our normal syllables for $O_n(\Z[\frac12])$
carry one sign where the paper's carry four, which removes most sign
cases from its Main Lemma. In the real Clifford+$CH$ appendix several
arguments were replaced by shorter ones found while formalizing: two
equations saying that a word of involutions equals its reverse, (146)
and (147), are proved by making the word a palindrome where the paper
draws some sixty diagram steps; a commutation of two multiply
controlled gates, (203), is proved letter by letter where the paper
takes three pages; the obligations of its Theorem~4.10 are the images
of one relation per rule under a uniform embedding, where the paper
does a case analysis over every rule and coset; and rule (45) of its
Lemma~8.8, an involution again, has a proof whose argument fits in a
page, where the paper's takes six. Each was checked numerically first
and then in Agda.

\paragraph{Normal forms.} The normal form of Cl\'ement's Lemma~A.4, a
sign part and a permutation part, was checked by enumeration, by a
script we did not keep, before its uniqueness was proved: at
$N=2,\dots,5$ the words number $2^{N-1}N!$ and no two have the same
matrix. The proof then reads the permutation part as a Lehmer code. And
the decision trees of Section~\ref{sec:trees} came out of a search
whose organization decided their size.

Whether the same scaffold helps to find a new theory, rather than to
check one, we do not know.
