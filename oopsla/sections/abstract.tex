Formalizing a proof used to take a person days or months. Language
models now write machine-checked proofs of hundreds of thousands of
lines, or millions. This moves the bottleneck. A formal proof proves its
statement, and someone still has to check that the statement and the
definitions under it mean what was intended. Generated code is not
written to be read, so that check is still done by people. We study how
to keep it small in one area of quantum computing: completeness of
equational theories for circuits. These papers have appendices of
dozens or hundreds of pages that referees have no time to check, and they share
one semantics, circuits read as matrices. On top of an Agda library
that fixes, once, what circuits are and what it means for relations to
present a group of matrices, an LLM coding agent formalized thirteen
completeness proofs written between 2014 and 2026, and the minimality
theorem of a fourteenth paper. For each paper a reader checks the
relations, the matrices of the gates and a one-line theorem. For real
stabilizer circuits that is under 500 of 11{,}799 lines. In all the
developments have about 300{,}000 lines of safe Agda without
postulates, on a library of 16{,}000 lines, and most of them were
written in 24 days.

Most of the proofs are correct, up to misprints that we list. One is
not. The completeness proof for Figure~6 of Hadamard-$\Pi$ (POPL 2026)
relies on two false lemmas, and its Main Lemma fails on an explicit
state in dimension~9. The theorem itself is true. We prove it by
formalizing the corresponding case of an unpublished 2019 thesis of
Cl\'ement, and then the whole thesis. Cl\'ement's 2026 completeness
theorem for real Clifford+$CH$ circuits, whose appendix has 264 pages,
imports this theorem; our proof of it has no such hypothesis. We
describe the proof architectures the papers share, how the proofs were
produced, and what they cost in Agda.
